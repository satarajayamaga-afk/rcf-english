Literary Techniques

Naming the technique earns one mark. Explaining what it does earns the rest. This page is mostly about the rest.

  1. Home
  2. O/L Literature
  3. Literary Techniques

The three-part rule

Whenever you write about a technique, write three things: name it, quote it, explain its effect.

Most students do the first two. The mark is in the third. The poet uses a metaphor is not analysis; the metaphor turns the gate's noise into a cry of pain, so the homecoming feels unwelcoming before anything has happened is.

Comparison and image

Simile
A comparison using like or as.
Example: Ask what the comparison brings with it. Comparing a crowd to a river suggests movement and force; comparing it to a herd suggests something else entirely.
Metaphor
A comparison that speaks of one thing as though it were another, without like or as.
Example: Stronger than a simile because it does not admit that it is a comparison. Ask why the writer wanted the identification to be complete.
Personification
Giving human qualities or actions to something not human.
Example: Ask why the writer wanted that thing to seem alive — usually to make it threatening, sympathetic, or watchful.
Imagery
Language appealing to the senses.
Example: Never write only "the writer uses imagery". Say what kind — visual, of sound, of touch — and what the images have in common.
Symbol
An object or action standing for a larger idea.
Example: Argue for your reading rather than assuming it. A symbol usually gathers meaning through repetition, so trace where it appears.
Contrast and juxtaposition
Two things placed side by side so that each shows up the other.
Example: A celebration described immediately after a funeral is not decoration; it is an argument being made by arrangement.

Sound and structure

Alliteration
Repetition of the same consonant sound at the start of nearby words.
Example: Say what the sound does: hard consonants can suggest force or anger, soft ones calm or secrecy.
Assonance
Repetition of vowel sounds within nearby words.
Example: Long vowels slow a line down; short ones quicken it.
Onomatopoeia
A word whose sound imitates its meaning — crash, hiss, murmur.
Example: Ask whether the sounds are pleasant or harsh, and what that suggests about the scene.
Repetition
A word, phrase or structure used again deliberately.
Example: It may suggest obsession, insistence, ritual or monotony. Say which — the word "emphasises" alone is too vague to score.
Enjambment
A sentence continuing past the end of a line without a pause.
Example: It can create speed or breathlessness, or spring a small surprise where the meaning turns at the line break.
Caesura
A strong pause within a line.
Example: It stops the reader mid-line, often at a moment of hesitation or shock.
Structure
How the whole text is arranged — stanzas, chapters, acts, time order.
Example: Structural comments are highly valued and rarely made. Ask why this text is in this order.

Meaning, voice and drama

Irony
A contrast between what is said or expected and what is actually the case.
Example: Distinguish verbal irony (saying the opposite of what is meant) from situational irony (an outcome opposite to what was expected).
Dramatic irony
The audience knows something a character does not.
Example: Say what it makes the audience feel — dread, pity, discomfort. That feeling is the effect you are being asked about.
Tone
The attitude the writing takes to its subject.
Example: Be precise. Not "sad", but wistful, bitter, resigned or defiant.
Mood or atmosphere
The feeling created in the reader.
Example: Tone belongs to the writer; mood belongs to the reader. Examination questions sometimes ask for one and receive the other.
Foreshadowing
An early hint of something that comes later.
Example: Once you know the ending, reread the opening. Foreshadowing is almost always there, and noticing it makes an answer look attentive.
Soliloquy and aside
A character alone speaking their thoughts; a short remark heard only by the audience.
Example: Both create intimacy between a character and the audience that other characters do not share.
Hyperbole
Deliberate exaggeration.
Example: Ask whether it is comic, desperate or self-deceiving — exaggeration usually tells you something about the speaker.
Understatement
Deliberately saying less than is meant.
Example: Often more powerful than exaggeration, especially about grief or fear, because the reader supplies what is left out.
