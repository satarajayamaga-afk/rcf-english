GCSE English Language

OCR GCSE English Language

Two papers of exactly the same shape, two hours each, eighty marks each. Once you see the pattern, OCR is the most predictable of the four boards.

Component 01Component 02
  1. Home
  2. Global English
  3. GCSE English
  4. OCR GCSE English Language

Where these figures come from

Every figure on this page was read from OCR's own published documents: the J351 specification and specification-at-a-glance, and the J351/01 and J351/02 mark schemes.

OCR's specification, papers and mark schemes are linked, never copied. No question, passage or mark-scheme wording is reproduced here beyond short factual phrases, and no paper-sharing site is linked.

RCF English is not affiliated with, endorsed by or approved by OCR.

OCR built J351 around a deliberate idea: the two papers mirror each other. Both last two hours, both are worth 80 marks and 50% of the grade, both split evenly into an hour of reading and an hour of writing, and both assess all six assessment objectives. Even the question shape is the same on each - four marks of short answers, a six-mark question, a twelve-mark question and an eighteen-mark comparison. What changes between them is the material: Component 01 gives you non-fiction across two centuries and asks for non-fiction writing; Component 02 gives you literary texts from the modern period and asks for creative writing. Learn the skeleton once and it works twice.

One choice a school makes that affects both papers

OCR states that the combination of texts in Component 02 depends on the combination chosen for Component 1. Component 02 uses prose fiction and/or literary non-fiction, and which mix appears is linked to what Component 01 used.

In practice this means the two papers are designed as a pair rather than independently, which is one more reason to prepare them together rather than one at a time.

What that grid tells you to do

Writing is half the qualification. AO5 and AO6 together are 50% - exactly as much as all four reading objectives combined. Students who treat the writing section as the thing they do after the reading are gambling with half their grade.

AO6 alone is 20%. A fifth of this GCSE is vocabulary range, sentence variety, spelling and punctuation. That is the most improvable 20% in the whole qualification and it needs no set text and no wider reading.

AO2 is the biggest reading objective at 18.75%, and it is concentrated in Component 02 (11.25% there against 7.5% in Component 01). If a student is weak at writing about language and structure, Component 02 is where it will cost most.

AO3, comparison, is only 7.5% - yet it feels like the hardest thing on the paper. It is worth six marks inside the eighteen-mark question on each paper. Do not let it eat revision time out of proportion to its weight.

The spoken language endorsement

Entered as J351/03 or J351/04. It is a compulsory requirement of the course, but it does not affect the GCSE grade.

  • One spoken presentation per candidate - a talk, debate, speech or dialogue - after which the student listens and responds to questions and feedback.
  • Internally assessed by the centre and externally monitored by OCR. A sample of up to 30 presentations must be video recorded for monitoring.
  • Reported as a separate grade: Pass, Merit, Distinction or Not Classified.
  • The marks do not contribute to the overall GCSE grade, and the endorsement appears separately on the certificate.
  • OCR notes that the assessment criteria and arrangements for spoken language are consistent across all examination boards - so guidance written for AQA or Edexcel applies here too.

Common questions about OCR English Language

Is OCR English Language easier than AQA?

No board is easier - grade boundaries are set so that the same standard earns the same grade. But the papers are shaped differently, and OCR's shape suits some students.

OCR's distinctive feature is symmetry: both papers run to the same pattern of 2, 2, 6, 12 and 18 marks in Section A, then a 40-mark writing task. AQA's two papers have different question structures from each other. A student who benefits from one repeated routine may find OCR easier to prepare for, even though the standard is the same.

Which paper has the 19th-century text?

Component 01. It gives two unseen non-fiction texts, one from the 19th century and one from the 20th or 21st.

Component 02 uses prose or literary non-fiction from the 20th and 21st centuries only - so if a student is anxious about older language, all of that pressure sits on Component 01.

Are the texts released in advance?

No. Both papers are entirely unseen, and the texts arrive in an Insert with the question paper. There are no set texts and nothing to learn by heart.

This is different from some other qualifications - Edexcel's iPrimary tests, for example, pre-release their texts. OCR GCSE does not.

How much choice is there in the writing section?

One task from a choice of two, on both papers. The tasks are connected to the theme of the reading section, and bullet points are provided for support.

On Component 01 the task will have a specified form, audience and purpose. On Component 02 it may be narrative, descriptive or autobiographical writing.

Do presentation and layout earn marks in the writing task?

No. OCR states plainly that presentational and visual features are not assessed - on either paper.

So a student writing a leaflet or an article should not spend time drawing boxes, headings and columns. The marks are for AO5 (24) and AO6 (16): communication, organisation, vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation.

What does the asterisk next to a question mean?

The paper states that **quality of extended response will be assessed in questions marked with an asterisk (*)**. In practice these are the longer questions, where how well the answer is constructed and expressed counts alongside what it says.

It is a signal to write in developed, connected paragraphs rather than in a list of separate points.
