RCF Model Paper: OCR Component 01

Two texts about the same building, written a hundred and sixty years apart. Five reading questions, one writing task, eighty marks, two hours.

  1. Home
  2. Global English

  6. RCF Model Paper: OCR Component 01

Unofficial original RCF practice material

This is not an OCR examination paper, past paper or sample assessment material. It is original practice material written by RCF English.

It follows the published structure of OCR GCSE (9-1) English Language J351, Component 01: Communicating information and ideas: 2 hours, 80 marks, two unseen non-fiction texts in an Insert, Section A reading worth 40 marks in questions of 2, 2, 6, 12 and 18 marks, and Section B writing worth 40 marks from a choice of two tasks.

Text 1 is an original composition written in a nineteenth-century voice. It is not the work of any nineteenth-century writer and is not an extract from any real speech, pamphlet or newspaper. Both texts were written for this paper.

RCF English is not affiliated with, endorsed by or approved by OCR. For official papers, mark schemes and examiners' reports see the past papers page.

Paper details

FieldDetail
Written byRCF English (original practice material)
Modelled onOCR GCSE (9-1) English Language, specification J351, Component 01
Component title it followsCommunicating information and ideas
Time allowed2 hours
Maximum mark80
SectionsSection A Reading, 40 marks. Section B Writing, 40 marks.
TextsText 1, nineteenth century. Text 2, twenty-first century. Both non-fiction, both original RCF compositions.
Theme linking the textsThe public library, and what people actually use it for
Section BOne task from a choice of two
AdviceAbout one hour on each section. Read both texts and all the questions before you begin.

Before you start

Work to time: 2 hours, no dictionary. The two hours are split evenly, so the 40-mark writing task is worth exactly as much as the whole reading section.

The marking guide is on a separate page. Do not open it until you have finished.

To print this paper, use your browser's print command and choose Save as PDF if you want a file.

A note on which text carries which question

On this paper the two short answers are set on Text 2 and the twelve-mark language question on Text 1. OCR may set them the other way round, so do not learn the order - learn the question types.

What never changes is that the six-mark question uses both texts and is about what they say, and the eighteen-mark question uses both texts and is about how well they say it.

Text 1

An original RCF composition written in a nineteenth-century voice. It is presented as an address to the ratepayers of a northern manufacturing town in 1861, reported in the local newspaper, in which a councillor argues that the town should adopt the Free Libraries Act.

The Question of a Free Library

1 Gentlemen, - I am aware that I have been described in this room as a man who wishes to spend your money upon novels, and I had better begin by admitting that I do wish to spend your money, though not upon novels.

4 The proposal before you is a penny in the pound. For that penny you would have a building, warmed and lighted, open from eight in the morning until ten at night, containing books which any man in this town might read without paying and without asking anybody's permission. I have heard three objections to it, and I shall take them in their order.

10 The first is that the working man does not want books. I would answer that in Manchester, where the experiment has been tried, the reading room was used last year by eleven thousand persons, of whom the greater number were working men, and that the committee there has twice been obliged to enlarge the premises. A want which has to be enlarged twice in four years is not an imaginary one.

17 The second objection is that he would read the wrong ones. I confess I find this the strangest of the three. We do not say of a man who has never eaten that he must not be fed until he can be trusted to choose his dinner. He will read what is put before him; what is put before him is a matter for the committee; and the committee, gentlemen, is you.

23 The third objection is the honest one, and it is the penny. I shall not pretend that the sum is nothing to a man who counts pennies, for I have counted them myself. But I would ask those gentlemen who feel it most keenly to consider what else the penny buys. It buys, in this town, a very great deal of gin. I am told that the public houses of this parish take more upon a single Saturday than the library would cost the parish in a year, and I have never once heard it argued in this room that the working man cannot afford that.

32 I will put the case at its lowest, since the highest has been laughed at. I do not say that a library will make men virtuous. I say that upon a wet evening in February a man who is out of work has, at present, the choice of a cold room or a warm public house, and that we propose to offer him a third door. Whether he goes through it is his affair. That it should be standing there is ours.

39 I move that the Act be adopted.

Text 2

An original RCF composition. It is presented as a piece written in 2024 by a librarian in a small branch library, published on a website that carries writing about working life.

The Warm Room

1 People ask what I do all day and I have stopped trying to answer, because the honest version sounds either boring or invented.

3 This morning, before eleven, I have renewed a large-print novel for a woman who has read every other one we own; explained twice that we cannot print in colour; found a man the form he needed and then, because the form was impossible, filled most of it in with him; told a toddler that we do not lick the dinosaur book; and helped a woman of about seventy send her first email, which was to her grandson, and which took forty minutes and was three lines long. In none of that did anybody borrow anything, and I would say all of it was library work.

12 The thing nobody outside the building quite understands is how little of this is about reading. We are the last indoor place in this town centre where a person can sit down for two hours without buying something. That is the whole of it, and everything else follows from that one fact: the man with the form, the students in September, the four regulars who arrive at ten past nine for the newspapers, the mother who comes in with a pushchair because it is raining and there is nowhere else to be.

20 In winter it is more obvious. We had a hundred and forty people through the door on the coldest day in January, and I know, because I was watching, that a good number of them took nothing at all off the shelves. Nobody says I am here because my heating is off. They say they are looking for something and cannot remember what it is called.

26 I am supposed to have opinions about this. The council would prefer the figures to be about issues - books out, books back - because issues can be counted and warmth cannot. And issues are down. I am not going to pretend otherwise. But I have started keeping a tally of my own, which is not official and would embarrass everybody if it were, of the number of conversations in a day that go on longer than the task required. It runs at about thirty.

34 I do not want to make this sound noble. Half of it is admin and a quarter of it is the printer. But when people write that libraries are finished because everybody has the internet at home, I would like to put them behind my desk for one Tuesday. Not everybody has the internet at home. Not everybody has a home that is warm at eleven in the morning. And there is nobody else left doing this, which is either an argument for us or an indictment of everything else, and most days I am too busy to work out which.

Section A: Reading

Answer all the questions in this section. You are advised to spend about 1 hour on this section. [40 marks]

Question 1 [2 marks]

Look again at lines 3 to 11 of Text 2.

Give two things the writer did in the library before eleven o'clock that morning.

[2 marks]

Question 2 [2 marks]

Look again at lines 12 to 25 of Text 2.

Explain two reasons the writer gives for saying that people come to the library for something other than books.

[2 marks]

Question 3 [6 marks]

Both writers describe people who use a library for something other than reading.

Using information from both texts, synthesise what the two writers say about these people and about why they come.

[6 marks]

Question 4 [12 marks]

Text 1 only.

Explore how the writer uses language and structure to persuade the ratepayers to vote for the library. Support your answer with reference to the text.

[12 marks]

Question 5 [18 marks]

Both texts.

"Neither of these writers is really arguing about books."

How far do you agree, and how convincingly does each writer make their case?

In your answer you should:

[18 marks]

  • compare the two writers' ideas and perspectives, and how these are conveyed;
  • evaluate how effectively each writer achieves what they set out to do;
  • support what you say with quotations from both texts.

How the 18 marks divide

Question 5 is marked in two parts which are added together: 12 marks for evaluating the texts critically and 6 marks for comparing the writers' ideas and perspectives.

Evaluation is worth twice comparison. An answer that compares the two texts neatly but never judges how well either writer does what they are trying to do is giving away twelve of the eighteen marks.

Section B: Writing

Answer one question in this section. You are advised to spend about 1 hour, including planning and checking. [40 marks]

Twenty-four marks are for content and organisation, and sixteen for vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation. Presentational features such as columns, boxes and headings are not assessed on this component, so do not spend time drawing them.

Either Question 6 [40 marks]

Your local council has proposed closing the branch library in your area.

Write a speech to be delivered at a public meeting, persuading the councillors present to keep it open.

In your speech you could write about:

[40 marks]

  • who uses the library, and what they use it for;
  • what would be lost if it closed;
  • the objections you expect, and your answers to them.

Or Question 7 [40 marks]

A website for young people is running a series called What that place is actually for.

Write an article for the site explaining to readers of your own age what a public library is for now, and whether it still matters.

In your article you could write about:

[40 marks]

  • what people your age think a library is;
  • what happens there that they may not know about;
  • what you think it is really for.

A timing plan for this paper

0-5 min read both texts once, quickly, for what each is about and who wrote it for whom.

5-10 min Questions 1 and 2 (4 marks). Do not spend fifteen minutes here; students routinely do.

10-18 min Question 3, synthesis (6 marks).

18-38 min Question 4, language and structure (12 marks).

38-60 min Question 5, comparison and evaluation (18 marks).

60-115 min Section B, including five minutes planning and five minutes checking.

115-120 min read your writing back for sentence endings, paragraph breaks and anything you were unsure how to spell.
