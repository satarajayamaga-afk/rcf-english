RCF Model Paper 1: Edexcel Marking Guide

Answers for all four reading questions, bands in plain English, and full model answers for the 15-mark evaluation and the 40-mark writing task.

  6. RCF Model Paper 1: Edexcel Marking Guide

Unofficial original RCF practice material

This marking guide accompanies RCF Model Paper 1, an original practice paper written by RCF English.

It is not a Pearson Edexcel mark scheme. The band descriptions are RCF English's own plain-English account of how marks divide; no Pearson mark scheme wording is reproduced. For the authoritative standard, use Pearson's mark schemes and exemplar scripts, linked on the papers page.

RCF English is not affiliated with, endorsed by or approved by Pearson Edexcel.

Questions 1 and 2: the short answers

QAnswerMarking
1 (1 mark)every Sunday evening after chapelOne mark for the phrase. Accept it with or without every. Do not accept a whole sentence copied out - the question asks for the phrase, and an answer that quotes lines 3 to 5 entire has not identified anything. Do not accept on a Sunday alone, which is not the phrase in the text.
2 (2 marks)One mark each, for any two of the following(a) He asks "Is he in the back?" - he expects Hannah's father to be there. (b) He behaves entirely as usual, shaking his hat over the mat as he "had always done". (c) He is described as "entirely happy". (d) He waits "for her to fetch her father as she had fetched him a hundred times before". (e) His greeting is cheerful small talk - "Filthy morning". Accept own words or quotation, as the question allows. Award no more than two.

Question 3: language and structure (6 marks)

Lines 25 to 43 only. Reward comment on both language and structure, supported by reference to the text. An answer that only discusses word choice is working at half the question.

Indicative content

  • Structure: the change is staged in three beats. He is still talking normally ("The little Swiss one"), then he stops mid-action, then he understands. The paragraph break before "Ah" isolates the moment of realisation on its own line.
  • "He began to unbutton his coat; and then, finding that she had still not spoken, he stopped with his fingers at the second button." - the interrupted action shows the change physically before it is stated. The second button is precise where a vaguer writer would have written stopped.
  • **The repeated *"She watched him"*** places the reader inside Hannah's view, so the change is observed rather than explained. We are told what she sees, not what he feels.
  • The clocks stopped at different hours - the detail "that gave it away". The writer lets Mr Bewley deduce the death from evidence, so the reader works it out at the same moment he does.
  • "It went over his face slowly, the way water finds its level" - the simile makes the understanding gradual and involuntary, something that happens to him rather than something he does.
  • "and his hand came down from his coat" - the smallest possible gesture used to close the sequence. The coat he was taking off is now staying on.
  • "Ah," he said. - one syllable given a paragraph. After the long, accumulating sentences before it, the shortness is the point: he has understood, and there is nothing to say.

Bands, in plain English

  • 5-6 Perceptive analysis of how language and structure work together; judicious short references; effects explained, not asserted.
  • 3-4 Clear explanation of some language and structural choices, with relevant references.
  • 1-2 Simple identification of features, with comment on effect asserted rather than explained.
  • 0 Nothing to reward, or an answer written on the wrong lines.

Question 4: evaluation (15 marks) - model answer

Original RCF model answer

Question: In this extract, there is an attempt to create strong feelings in the reader. Evaluate how successfully this is achieved. Support your views with detailed reference to the text.

The extract is unusually successful at producing strong feeling, and it manages it by consistently refusing to ask for any. Almost everything that might have been made emotional is instead made factual. Hannah's grief is never named; we are told that the shop was quiet, that the clocks were not going, and that nobody had wound them. The reader is left to supply the feeling, and because the reader supplies it, it is felt rather than observed.

The opening is the clearest example. "The shop had never, in Hannah's memory, been quiet, and the quiet was the worst of it." This is a bereavement described entirely through sound, or its absence, and the sentence that follows - "Forty-one clocks stood along the two long walls, and not one of them was going" - is delivered as though it were an inventory. The precision of forty-one does the work: an approximate number would have sounded like scene-setting, while an exact one sounds like someone who has counted them, which tells us how long she has been standing in that room. The image of her father winding them, "his lips moving as he counted", is affectionate without a single affectionate word in it.

The most effective decision, though, is the choice of what finally breaks her. The writer sets up an obvious candidate and then discards it. We are told that Hannah has already said the words to the minister, to the dairy woman and twice to strangers, and that each time "they had come out quite steadily". What defeats her is not sympathy but its opposite: a customer who does not know, asking a cheerful, ordinary question. "Is he in the back?" is unbearable precisely because it is so unremarkable, and because the reader knows the answer before Hannah has to give it. The sustained, comfortable description of Mr Bewley - "a large, comfortable man", "entirely happy" - makes his ignorance worse, not funnier.

Some readers may find the moment of realisation slightly over-managed. "It went over his face slowly, the way water finds its level" is a fine simile, but it is the one point at which the writer explains an effect that the surrounding detail had already achieved; the stopped clocks at different hours had made the deduction clear a line earlier. It is a small failure of nerve in a passage that otherwise trusts the reader entirely.

That reservation aside, the ending is where the extract earns its feeling. It would have been easy to close on Hannah weeping. Instead the writer gives us a single practical action - she winds one clock - and then one sentence of restrained hope: "But she came to it again on the Sunday evening, after chapel; and after a while she began upon the others." The return to the Sunday routine of the opening closes the extract on the father's habit rather than on his absence, and the phrase after a while concedes honestly that this took time. Because the writer has withheld emotional language throughout, this quiet ending carries far more weight than a scene of open grief would have done. The attempt is not merely successful; the restraint is the reason it succeeds.

Why this answer works

  • It evaluates from the first sentence. Unusually successful ... by consistently refusing to ask for any is a judgement with a reason attached. Question 4 is AO4 - evaluate - and answers that describe the extract well but never judge it are capped in the middle bands however good the analysis.
  • It has a thesis and tests it. The claim is that restraint produces the feeling, and every paragraph returns to it. This is what turns four separate points into an argument.
  • The reservation is what lifts it into the top band. Some readers may find the moment of realisation slightly over-managed is a genuine criticism, argued with evidence, in an answer that is otherwise positive. Evaluation means weighing, and an answer that only praises has not weighed anything.
  • References are short and embedded - a phrase at a time, never a copied sentence. Fifteen marks in about twenty-five minutes leaves no room for long quotation.
  • Method is analysed, not just noticed: why forty-one rather than about forty; why the interruption comes from a customer rather than a mourner; why the ending returns to Sunday.
  • The final sentence answers the question directly. The attempt is not merely successful; the restraint is the reason it succeeds. Examiners look for a clear overall verdict, and it should be the last thing they read.

Questions 5 and 6: imaginative writing (40 marks)

Marked as 24 marks for AO5 (communication and organisation) and 16 marks for AO6 (vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation). Mark the two separately, and mark AO5 first.

AO5, 24 marks - communication and organisation

  • 19-24 Convincing and compelling; the form and purpose are sustained throughout; structure is deliberate and controlled, with paragraph length varied for effect; the reader is held from the first line.
  • 13-18 Consistent and clear; well organised; a recognisable shape with a considered opening and ending.
  • 7-12 Some success; a coherent piece with lapses in control or organisation.
  • 1-6 Simple and limited; little structure; the reader has to work to follow it.
  • 0 Nothing to reward.

AO6, 16 marks - vocabulary and accuracy

  • 13-16 A wide range of sentence forms and vocabulary used consciously for effect; demanding punctuation used accurately; spelling secure.
  • 9-12 A range of sentence forms; mostly accurate punctuation and spelling.
  • 5-8 Some variety; errors present but meaning is clear throughout.
  • 1-4 Limited range; errors impede the reader.
  • 0 Not enough evidence to assess.
  • Remember these questions are asterisked. AO6 is 40% of this task's marks, which is more than most candidates assume.

Question 6: model answer

Original RCF model answer - about 520 words

Question: Write about a time when you, or someone you know, was shown unexpected kindness. Your response could be real or imagined.

The bus had gone. I want to be clear that this was not a small thing, because it was the last one, and the next was at ten past six the following morning, and I was fourteen and forty minutes from anywhere.

I had been told to be at the stop by half past five. I had been told twice. What I had done instead was stand in the doorway of the newsagent reading the back of a magazine I had no intention of buying, and the bus had come along the far side of the green, and by the time I understood that the noise was the bus it was already the sound of the bus leaving.

There is a particular silence that follows a mistake like that. It is not really silence - there was a dog somewhere, and the traffic on the bypass - but everything arranges itself around the fact of what you have done, and the fact sits in the middle and hums.

I sat on the bench. It began, in a leisurely and unhurried way, to rain.

The woman who ran the newsagent came out at about six. I had seen her a hundred times and never once thought about her; she was simply the person behind the counter, in the way that at fourteen you assume adults are permanent fixtures of the buildings they stand in. She locked the door, tried the handle, and then looked at the bench in a way that suggested she had been aware of me for some time.

"Missed it," she said. It was not a question.

I said that I had.

She did not tell me that I should have been paying attention, which I had been braced for and would have deserved. She did not ask why I had not telephoned anyone, which would have required me to admit that I had no money for the box. She stood in the rain, in a coat that was clearly not meant for it, and thought for a moment, and then she said that she went past our village on her way home and that it was no trouble.

It was, of course, entirely trouble. I worked out later, with the particular shame of working something out too late, that it added eleven miles to her journey.

In the car she asked me nothing at all. She put the radio on, at the volume adults use when they want you to understand that you are not required to talk, and she drove, and once she said that the rain was set in for the night, and I said that it looked like it, and that was the whole of our conversation. She stopped at the end of our lane rather than at the house, which I understood even then was a kindness of a different and more precise kind - she had guessed that I would rather my mother did not see me delivered.

I said thank you. I meant it, but I said it the way a fourteen-year-old says it, which is to say inadequately and while already getting out of the car.

She was there for years afterwards. I bought sweets from her, and later newspapers, and I never once mentioned it, and neither did she. I have thought about that far more than I have thought about the bus.

Why this answer works

  • AO5, top band. The narrative covers about forty minutes and one car journey - a deliberately small canvas, closely observed. The commonest failure in GCSE imaginative writing is trying to cover too much; the commonest feature of top-band work is a narrow subject given full attention.
  • The structure is shaped, not chronological by accident. It opens after the event (The bus had gone), fills in the mistake, holds still on the bench, brings in the woman, and closes years later. The final paragraph reframes the whole piece.
  • Paragraph length is used for effect. I sat on the bench. It began, in a leisurely and unhurried way, to rain. is a two-sentence paragraph placed between two long ones, and it is the pivot of the piece.
  • **The kindness is shown through what is not done** - she does not lecture, does not ask, stops at the lane rather than the house. This is far more affecting than describing how kind she was, and it gives the reader something to infer.
  • AO6, top band. Sentence forms vary deliberately: "Missed it," she said. It was not a question. against the long accumulating sentence about the silence. Punctuation includes semicolons, dashes and dialogue, all accurate.
  • Precise, unshowy vocabulary: leisurely and unhurried, permanent fixtures, inadequately. No thesaurus words, and no similes for their own sake.
  • Honest self-assessment in the narrator's voice - the particular shame of working something out too late - which reads as a real perspective rather than a school exercise.
  • About 520 words. Around 450 to 600 is the practical range in 45 minutes. Longer pieces lose AO6 marks faster than they gain AO5 ones.
