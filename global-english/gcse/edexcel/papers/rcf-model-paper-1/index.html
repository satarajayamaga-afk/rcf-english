RCF Model Paper 1: Edexcel English Language

One nineteenth-century fiction extract, four questions worth twenty-four marks, and forty marks for a piece of your own. One hour and forty-five minutes.

Unofficial original RCF practice material

This is not a Pearson Edexcel examination paper, past paper or sample assessment material. It is original practice material written by RCF English.

It follows the published structure of Pearson Edexcel GCSE English Language 1EN0, Paper 1: Fiction and Imaginative Writing: 1 hour 45 minutes, 64 marks, one unseen nineteenth-century fiction extract, four reading questions worth 1, 2, 6 and 15 marks, and one imaginative writing task worth 40 marks from a choice of two.

The source text is an original composition written in a nineteenth-century voice. It is not the work of any nineteenth-century writer and is not an extract from any real novel or publication. It was written for this paper.

RCF English is not affiliated with, endorsed by or approved by Pearson Edexcel. For official papers see the papers page.

Before you start

Work to time: 1 hour 45 minutes. In the real examination the extract arrives in a separate Reading Text Insert; here it is printed below the paper details.

The marking guide is on a separate page. Do not open it until you have finished.

To print this paper, use your browser's print command and choose Save as PDF if you want a file.

Reading Text

An original RCF composition written in a nineteenth-century voice. It is presented as an extract from a novel published in 1879. It is not by any real author and is not taken from any real book.

From The Long Case

1 The shop had never, in Hannah's memory, been quiet, and the quiet was the worst of it. Forty-one clocks stood along the two long walls, and not one of them was going. Her father had wound them every Sunday evening after chapel, moving down the row with the key held out before him and his lips moving as he counted; and nobody had wound them since.

6 She had taken down the shutters that morning because she could not think what else to do. It was four days. Her aunt had said that the shop ought to stay shut until the month was out, and her aunt was very likely right; but the house behind the shop had become insupportable, and there was a kind of mercy in the front room, with its smell of oil and brass and its window full of ordinary weather.

12 On the bench under the window her father's work lay exactly as he had left it: a movement in pieces on a square of green baize, the parts set out in the order they came away, and beside them a scrap of paper with two figures written on it in pencil. She had not touched any of it. She did not know what the figures meant and there was no longer anybody who did.

17 At twenty minutes past ten the bell above the door went, and Mr Bewley came in out of the rain, shaking his hat over the mat in the way he had always done and that her father had always minded.

20 "Filthy morning," he said. "Filthy. Is he in the back?"

21 He was a large, comfortable man who had brought his watch to be regulated twice a year for eleven years, and he stood dripping in the doorway with his eyebrows up, entirely happy, waiting for her to fetch her father as she had fetched him a hundred times before.

25 Hannah found that she could not do it. She had said the words to the minister, and to the woman from the dairy, and twice to strangers in the street, and every time they had come out quite steadily; and now they would not come at all. She stood behind the counter with her hands flat upon it and said nothing whatever.

30 "The little Swiss one," Mr Bewley said, helpfully. "He said Thursday." He began to unbutton his coat; and then, finding that she had still not spoken, he stopped with his fingers at the second button.

33 She watched him look about the shop. She watched him see the clocks - all of them stopped, and stopped at different hours, which was the detail that gave it away, for a shop that was merely shut for the day would have had them stopped together. She saw the moment when he understood. It went over his face slowly, the way water finds its level, and his hand came down from his coat.

39 "Ah," he said.

40 That was the whole of it, and it undid her completely. She had borne the minister, and she had borne the dairy woman, and she had borne being told, four separate times, that it was a mercy; and she was undone by a large wet man saying Ah in the middle of her father's shop.

44 He did not come round the counter, which she was grateful for afterwards, and he did not say any of the things. He stood where he was until she had finished. Then he asked, in an ordinary voice, whether she would rather he went; and she said no.

48 When he did go - an hour later, having drunk a cup of tea standing up, and having said nothing further about the watch - she took the key from the drawer and wound the long case clock beside the door. It was the only one she wound that day. But she came to it again on the Sunday evening, after chapel; and after a while she began upon the others.

Section A: Reading

Read the text above and answer all questions.

You should spend about 1 hour on this section. [24 marks]

Question 1

From lines 1 to 5, identify the phrase which describes when Hannah's father wound the clocks.

(Total for Question 1 = 1 mark)

Question 2

From lines 17 to 24, give two ways in which the writer shows that Mr Bewley does not yet know what has happened.

You may use your own words or quotations from the text.

(Total for Question 2 = 2 marks)

Question 3

In lines 25 to 43, how does the writer use language and structure to show the change in Mr Bewley?

Support your views with reference to the text.

(Total for Question 3 = 6 marks)

Question 4

In this extract, there is an attempt to create strong feelings in the reader.

Evaluate how successfully this is achieved.

Support your views with detailed reference to the text.

(Total for Question 4 = 15 marks)

TOTAL FOR SECTION A = 24 MARKS

Section B: Imaginative Writing

Answer ONE question.

You should spend about 45 minutes on this section.

EITHER — Question 5*

Look at the images described below. Write about a place of work that mattered to someone.

Your response could be real or imagined. You may wish to base your response on one of the images.

*Your response will be marked for the accurate and appropriate use of vocabulary, spelling, punctuation and grammar.

(Total for Question 5 = 40 marks)

OR — Question 6*

Write about a time when you, or someone you know, was shown unexpected kindness.

Your response could be real or imagined.

*Your response will be marked for the accurate and appropriate use of vocabulary, spelling, punctuation and grammar.

(Total for Question 6 = 40 marks)

TOTAL FOR SECTION B = 40 MARKS

TOTAL FOR PAPER = 64 MARKS

About the images in Question 5

The real Edexcel paper supplies two photographs with Question 5. Photographs cannot be reproduced here without a licence, so this paper describes two in words instead. The task itself is unchanged.

Image A - the inside of a small workshop. A wooden bench under a window, tools hung in outlines painted on the wall, sawdust on the floor, an apron over the back of a chair. Nobody is in the room.

Image B - a woman in her sixties standing behind the counter of a shop, mid-conversation with someone out of frame, hands resting on the counter. Shelves of stock behind her, a handwritten sign taped to the till.

If you would rather not work from a description, answer Question 6 instead. Both are worth the same 40 marks and are marked identically.

End of RCF Model Paper 1

Total: 64 marks. Section A carries 24 marks and Section B carries 40 marks.

The marking guide gives the answers, indicative content for every question, plain-English bands, a full model answer for Question 4 and a model piece of imaginative writing.

This paper is unofficial original RCF practice material and is not a Pearson Edexcel examination paper.
