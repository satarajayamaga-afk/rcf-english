ESL Teaching Guide

How to Make an ESL Lesson Plan

A lesson plan is not a form to fill in for an inspector. It is the set of decisions you make before the lesson so that you do not have to make them in front of thirty people.

  • Lesson planning
  • Teacher development
  • For any level
  • Large classes
  1. Home
  2. Global English
  3. ESL Teaching Resources
  4. How to Make an ESL Lesson Plan

Why bother

This guide takes you through those decisions in the order they are actually made, with the wording to use, the two mistakes that cause most weak lessons, and a blank plan you can copy.

Advertisement

1. Start at the end: what will they be able to do?

Write the aim as something learners will be able to do, not as something you will cover. "Present perfect" is a topic, not an aim. "Learners will be able to ask about and describe life experiences with Have you ever ...?" is an aim, because you can see whether it happened.

The test is simple: at the end of the lesson, could you point at a learner and say whether they can do it? If not, the aim is too vague to plan from.

In practice

Write the aim first and the activities second. Most weak lessons are a list of activities with an aim added afterwards.

2. Decide what the language actually is

Take the language you are teaching and look at three things before the lesson, not during it: what it means, how it is formed, and how it sounds.

Write two or three questions that check the meaning without using the grammar name. For the past simple: Is it finished? Do we know when? These are concept-checking questions, and they save you from the answer every teacher has heard - yes, teacher - when you ask "do you understand?"

Then write down what your learners will get wrong, and what you will do about each one. A problem you have predicted takes a minute to fix; one that surprises you takes ten.

In practice

If you cannot say what the language means in one plain sentence, you are not ready to teach it yet.

3. Choose a shape for the lesson

PPP - presentation, practice, production - suits a new, small piece of language: you show it, they practise it, they use it. It is the easiest shape to control with a large class.

Test-teach-test suits language learners have met before: let them try it first, see what is broken, teach that, then let them try again. Better than PPP when the class half-knows something.

Task-based suits a class that needs to do something real - make a plan, solve a problem - with the language taught where it is needed. It gives the most speaking and needs the most confidence.

Whichever you choose, controlled practice comes before free practice. Learners cannot be accurate and creative at the same time.

In practice

One shape per lesson. Mixing two is how a lesson loses its direction halfway through.

4. Give every stage a time and an interaction pattern

Beside each stage write the minutes and who talks to whom: whole class, individual, pairs, groups, mingle. Two lines in a plan, and they prevent the lesson where the teacher talks for forty minutes.

Add up the times. If the total is longer than the lesson - and it will be, the first few times - cut a stage rather than hurry all of them.

Aim to have learners speaking to each other for at least half the lesson. In a class of forty, that is the only way most of them speak at all.

In practice

Plan the change of pattern, not just the activity: "in pairs" written down is a decision; decided in the moment, it becomes "work with your friend" and chaos.

5. Write the instructions you will say

Write the three or four sentences you will actually use to set each task, in the shortest words you can. Long instructions are the commonest cause of a class not doing what you asked.

Then write one instruction-checking question for any task that could be misunderstood: Do you write or speak? Do you work alone or with a partner? How long have you got?

Plan to demonstrate rather than explain wherever you can. Doing one round with a strong learner is worth three sentences of description.

In practice

Give the instruction, then hand out the paper. Do it the other way round and nobody hears the instruction.

6. Plan how you will know it worked

Decide before the lesson how you will check the aim: an exit task of two or three sentences, a question round the room, or a task you collect. Without this, "they seemed to understand" is all you will have.

Keep it small. Three sentences written in the last four minutes tell you more than a page of homework marked a week later.

Plan the feedback too: note mistakes while they speak, put four sentences on the board at the end - two right, two wrong - and let the class repair them. Nobody is named, and everybody learns.

In practice

Whatever your exit task is, it should be in the form the aim describes. If the aim was questions, the exit task asks for questions.

7. Plan for the class you actually have

Too many learners: turn any mingle into rows - ask the four people nearest you - and collect written work from one row each lesson rather than all of them.

No photocopier: put the text on the board before the lesson, or use dictation; learners write in their notebooks. Almost no good activity truly needs paper.

Mixed levels: plan one support - a list of the verbs, a sentence frame - and one stretch - an extra question, a harder round. Two lines in the plan, and both ends of the class have something to do.

The lesson runs short: keep one five-minute speaking activity in your pocket that needs no materials. The end of a lesson is not the time to invent one.

In practice

A plan that ignores your real class is a plan you will abandon in week two.

A blank plan to copy

Everything below fits on one side of paper. A plan you cannot see at a glance is a plan you will not look at while teaching.

PartWhat goes in it
Class and dateWho, when, how long
LevelCEFR level, or the course level
Main aimBy the end of the lesson, learners will be able to ...
Subsidiary aimsOne or two, in the same form
Target languageThe sentences themselves, not the grammar name
Meaning, form, pronunciationConcept questions; the patterns; the sounds
Anticipated problemsWhat they will get wrong, and your solution for each
MaterialsEverything you must bring or prepare
StagesStage, aim, time, interaction, what happens
Support and stretchOne line each
Checking learningThe exit task
HomeworkIf any

Five mistakes that weaken a lesson

The mistakeWhat it looks like, and the fix
Planning activities instead of an aimThe lesson is busy and pleasant, and at the end nobody can do anything they could not do before. Fix it by writing the aim first, and asking of every activity: does this serve it?
Too much teacher talkThe plan has no interaction patterns, so everything defaults to teacher-to-class. Write the pattern beside every stage and count the minutes learners speak.
Free practice too earlyLearners are asked to use language they have not yet used accurately even once, so they avoid it. Put a controlled stage in between.
Instructions invented on the spotThe class does the wrong thing, and five minutes disappear. Write the words, demonstrate, and check with one question.
No way of knowing whether it workedPlan the exit task when you plan the aim, not when the bell is about to go.
