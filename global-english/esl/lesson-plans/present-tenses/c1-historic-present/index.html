Historic Present ESL Lesson Plan (C1, 60 Minutes)

A complete 60-minute lesson on the historic present for C1 classes, built on the TBL framework, with photocopiable handouts and an answer key.

About this lesson

Advanced students with a strong command of tenses. They tell stories in the past tense only and are not aware of how and why present forms are used for dramatic effect, live commentary, headlines and summaries.

Everything the lesson needs is on this page: the language analysis to read before you teach, a stage-by-stage procedure with timings and interaction patterns, and the handouts and answer key at the end, ready to print.

At a glance

Level
C1 (Advanced)
Learners
Teenagers and adults
Length
60 minutes. For 45 minutes, drop the commentary in Part B and keep the anecdote, then go straight to the analysis.
Skills
Speaking, with reading and some writing
Language focus
Present forms used for past and timeless reference: the historic present in anecdote, live commentary, headlines and plot summary
Framework
TBL (Task-Based Learning)
Materials
Handout 1 (pre-task headlines), Handout 2 (task instructions and story prompts), Handout 3 (texts for language analysis), Handout 4 (practice), phones for recording (optional), a short silent video clip (sport or cooking, 60–90 seconds) or a set of sequenced images.
Preparation
About 20 minutes: print Handouts 1 to 4 per learner and find a silent video clip of 60 to 90 seconds, or print six images in sequence. Prepare your own one-minute anecdote and decide where you will switch tense.

Aims

Main aim. To develop students' ability to recognise and use present forms for dramatic narrative (the historic present), live commentary, newspaper headlines and plot summaries.

Learning outcome. By the end of the lesson, students can tell a short anecdote in the historic present, deliver a 60-second live commentary and write headlines and a plot summary using appropriate present forms.

  • Subsidiary aim. To develop fluency and vividness in spoken anecdotes.
  • Subsidiary aim. To raise awareness of genre conventions.

Language analysis

What to know before the lesson: the meaning, form and pronunciation of the target language, and the problems learners usually have with it.

Uses of present forms with past or general reference

GenreTypical formsExample
Spoken anecdote (historic present)Present continuous for background; present simple for main eventsSo I'm walking home and this man comes up to me and says ...
Live commentaryPresent simple for quick completed actions; continuous for longer onesHe passes to Kumar, Kumar shoots ... and it's in! The crowd is going wild.
Newspaper headlinesPresent simple for past events; to + infinitive for future; past participle for passiveMinister resigns over scandal · President to visit Jaffna · Three injured in crash
Plot summaries, reviews and reporting what writers arguePresent simpleIn the novel, the hero returns home ... · Smith argues that ...
JokesPresent simple / continuousA man walks into a bar ...

Why use the present?

  • It creates immediacy and drama: the listener feels the action is happening now.
  • Speakers often switch from past to present at the most exciting moment of a story.
  • Headlines use the present to save space and to make news feel current.
  • Summaries use the present because the story "exists" in the text permanently.

Discourse features of anecdotes

  • Fillers and markers: So ..., Anyway ..., And then ..., Next thing I know ..., Suddenly ...
  • Reporting with says / goes (informal): And he goes, "Excuse me, is this your bag?"

Pronunciation

  • Commentary: fast pace, rising pitch at climaxes, stress on names and key verbs.
  • Anecdotes: pausing before the key moment for effect.

Anticipated problems and solutions

ProblemSolution
Students mix tenses randomly rather than for effect.In analysis, highlight where switches happen and why. Rule of thumb: start in the past, switch to the present for the drama, and stay there.
Students don't see the historic present as "correct" English.Show authentic-style examples from speech, journalism and literature. Discuss register: very common in speech, less common in formal written narrative.
Over-use of goes for says in formal contexts.Point out it is informal speech only.
Weak students struggle to produce commentary in real time.Let them watch the clip once first and note verbs; allow pairs to share the commentary.
No video available.Use a sequence of six images (e.g. a cricket shot or a cooking process) displayed one by one.

Anecdote · Commentary · Headline · Plot summary · Joke

CEFR C1 · 60 minutes · Teenagers and adults

Procedure: Historic Present, step by step

Pre-task8 min · Whole class, then pairs

Aim: To introduce the topic, activate schemata and set up the task.

Give out Handout 1. Say: "Here are six newspaper headlines. With a partner, guess the full story behind each one." (3 minutes.)

Take some guesses. Ask: "Did these events happen in the past or are they happening now?" (Past.) "So why are the verbs in the present?" Do not explain yet; say "We'll come back to that."

Tell a short anecdote yourself (about 1 minute), switching into the historic present at the dramatic moment. For example: "Last week I was at the market. It was busy. So I'm standing at the fish stall, and this man comes up behind me and grabs my bag ..."

Ask: "Did you notice anything about how I told the story?" Accept ideas.

Set the task. Say: "Today you're going to be storytellers and commentators. First, tell a partner a true or invented story, and make it as dramatic as possible. Then you will do a live commentary." Give out Handout 2.

Task15 min · Pairs, then groups of four

Aim: To give students the opportunity to use language freely for a real communicative purpose.

Part A, anecdote (7 minutes). Each student chooses a prompt from Handout 2 (a lost item, a surprise, an embarrassing moment, a near miss). They have one minute to think, then tell their story to a partner in about two minutes. The partner listens and asks two questions.

ICQs: "How long is your story?" (About two minutes.) "What does your partner do?" (Listens and asks two questions.)

Part B, commentary (8 minutes). Play the silent video (or show the images). Say: "Watch once. Now, in your group of four, take turns to give a 60-second live commentary while the video plays again. Record it on your phone if you can."

Monitor throughout without correcting. Note language used (or avoided) for later analysis.

Planning7 min · Groups

Aim: To give students time to prepare a polished version for the class, focusing on accuracy and impact.

Say: "Each group chooses the best story and the best commentary. Help the speakers make them even more dramatic and accurate. You will present to the class."

Groups improve the story and commentary. They may write key sentences. Help with language on request but do not teach the grammar point yet.

Report8 min · Whole class

Aim: To let groups share their work and to provide a purpose for listening.

Each group presents one story or one commentary (about 1 minute each).

Listening task for the class: "Which story was the most dramatic? Why?"

Board two or three sentences you heard that used present forms effectively, and one that did not.

Ask: "When did the speakers switch to the present? What was the effect?"

Language focus, analysis12 min · Groups, then whole class

Aim: To raise awareness of the forms and functions of the present in the different genres.

Give out Handout 3 (four extracts: anecdote, commentary, headlines, plot summary). Groups answer the analysis questions (6 minutes).

Feedback as a class. Build the centre of the board: which forms appear in each genre, and why.

Discuss:

Where does the speaker switch from past to present in Extract 1? Why there?

What do the headlines leave out? (Articles, auxiliaries.)

Why is the plot summary in the present?

Highlight the anecdote language (right of the board) and its informal register.

Language focus, practice10 min · Individual, then pairs

Aim: To practise the forms accurately.

Students complete Handout 4, Exercises A and B (6 minutes).

Pairs compare. Check answers as a class.

Exercise C (headline writing): pairs write three headlines for stories told in the lesson and read them out. The class guesses which story each refers to.

Homework Write a 200-word synopsis of a film or book using the present tense, and three headlines about real events from this week.

TBL (Task-Based Learning). Stage times add up to 60 minutes.

Differentiation

Support
Provide the anecdote language as a written list during the task. Let weaker students tell their story first in the past, then retell it in the present with a partner's help.
Stretch
Stronger students compare the historic present with free indirect speech in a short literary extract. They write a plot summary of a film using the present simple and at least two participle clauses.

Adapting the lesson

ForWhat to change
A large class (40 or more)Keep the anecdote in pairs and the commentary in fours, both at the same time, and hear only four or five in the Report stage. The analysis works as a whole-class discussion from the board.
No photocopierWrite the six headlines on the board and read the four extracts aloud twice, pausing for learners to note the verb forms. The commentary needs no handout at all once the task is set.
An online classScreen-share the silent clip and mute yourself during the commentary so learners can hear each other. Ask them to record the commentary on their phones and send you one file per group.
A different age groupWith teenagers, use a sports clip and let the commentary be the main task. With adults, add the professional uses of the present: summarising a report, a film or what a writer argues.

Checking learning

Students switch to the present deliberately at the climax of their story. The commentary uses the present simple for quick actions and the continuous for longer ones. Students can explain why headlines and summaries use the present. 8 out of 10 or better on Handout 4.

Teacher's reflection

Did the task generate a natural need for the target language? Did students use the present in the Report stage more effectively than in the first Task? Was the analysis stage long enough?

Part A: Tell a story

Choose one prompt. Take one minute to think. Then tell your partner your story in about two minutes. Make it dramatic! Your partner listens and asks two questions at the end.

Part B: Live commentary

Watch the video once. Then, in your group, take turns to give a 60-second live commentary while the video plays again. Imagine you are on the radio: the listeners cannot see anything!

Questions

  • Find a present continuous verb in Extract 1. What does it describe: background or main events? ___________________________
  • In Extract 2, why does the commentator use the present simple for runs, bowls, pulls? ___________________________

The text

It was last December, and I was flying home from Dubai. Everything was normal. So I'm sitting there, I'm reading my book, and suddenly the plane drops, like, a hundred metres. People are screaming, drinks are flying everywhere. And the man next to me turns to me, totally calm, and goes, "Is this your first time on a plane?"

And he runs in, he bowls ... it's a short ball, and Perera pulls it ... it's high, it's long, and it's going all the way! That's six! The crowd are on their feet, they're singing, they're dancing in the stands!

NEW BRIDGE TO OPEN IN MAY

PAINTING STOLEN FROM NATIONAL MUSEUM

The text

Answer Key

Handout 1: The headlines use the present simple for past events (1, 2, 4, 5), to + infinitive for a future plan (3) and set to for a likely future (6). The present makes the news feel current and saves space.

Common errors to watch for

  • Past: was, was flying, was. The switch happens at So I'm sitting there, just before the dramatic event, to make it feel immediate.
  • I'm sitting, I'm reading, are screaming, are flying: background and ongoing actions.
  • They are quick, completed actions happening as the commentator speaks.
  • Articles and auxiliaries are missing (A storm has hit the coast; The new bridge is to open; A painting has been stolen). To open = will open (future plan). Stolen = passive.
  • Plot summaries conventionally use the present because the story exists permanently in the text.
  • Extract 1: So, like, totally, goes.
  • President visits flood area · 2. Government to build new airport · 3. Two hurt in fire · 4. In the film, a young girl finds a lost dog and looks for its owner. · 5. He hits the ball and it goes for six! · 6. So I'm walking home and this woman stops me.
ErrorCorrection
(Plot summary) At the end, Hamlet died.At the end, Hamlet dies.
Mixing tenses randomly within one scene.Switch tense once, at the dramatic moment, and stay in the present.
Prime Minister is resigning today (headline).Prime Minister resigns / Prime Minister to resign.
Using goes for says in a formal essay.Use says or a reporting verb in formal writing.
