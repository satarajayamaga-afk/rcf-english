Formal Quantity Expressions and Agreement ESL Lesson Plan (C1, 60 Minutes)

A complete 60-minute C1 lesson on Formal Quantity Expressions and Agreement, built round the theme "Reporting Survey Results".

About this lesson

Who it is for. Advanced students who use everyday quantifiers accurately but rely on a lot of and many in formal writing. They make agreement errors (The number of students have increased, A large amount of people), and they misuse less / fewer and amount / number.

Everything the lesson needs is on this page: the language analysis, the timed stages with the teacher's own words and instruction-checking questions, the differentiation, the printable handouts and the answer key.

At a glance

Level
CEFR C1 (Advanced)
Learners
Teenagers and adults
Length
60 minutes. For 45 minutes, run "Freer practice" once through without the second round and cut "Feedback and wrap-up" to board-based error correction.
Skills
Speaking and writing
Language focus
Grammar: Formal Quantity Expressions and Agreement in Report Writing (the majority of, a significant proportion of, the number of vs a number of)
Framework
PPP (presentation, practice, production)
Materials
Handout 1 (an extract from a survey report), Handout 2 (language analysis), Handout 3 (practice), Handout 4 (survey data for writing).
Preparation
About 10 minutes: print Handouts 1 to 4, one per learner, and read the language analysis. The answer key is for you, not the class.

Aims

Main aim. To develop students' accurate use of formal quantity expressions for countable and uncountable nouns (a large / significant number of, a great deal of, a substantial amount of, the (vast) majority of, a (small) minority of, a handful of, a significant proportion of, scarcely any) and subject–verb agreement, including the number of vs a number of.

Learning outcome. By the end of the lesson, students can describe survey data accurately and formally, e.g. The vast majority of respondents (87%) own a smartphone, whereas only a small minority read printed newspapers. The number of students who read for pleasure has fallen.

  • Subsidiary aim. To accurately describe survey data with fractions and percentages.
  • Subsidiary aim. To write a short report paragraph.
Advertisement

Language analysis

What to know before the lesson: the meaning, form and pronunciation of the target language, and the problems learners usually have with it.

Formal quantity expressions

Countable (plural) nounsUncountable nounsBoth
a large / significant / growing number of + plurala great deal of / a substantial amount of / a considerable amount ofthe (vast) majority of · a (small) minority of
a handful ofa large quantity ofa significant proportion / percentage of
fewer / (very) few / scarcely anyless / (very) little / scarcely anyalmost all · hardly any · plenty of (less formal)

Agreement

  • a number of + plural noun → plural verb: A number of students have complained. (= several)
  • the number of + plural noun → singular verb: The number of students has increased. (the subject is the number)
  • the majority / a minority / a proportion / percentage of + plural noun → usually plural verb: The majority of respondents were satisfied. With an uncountable or singular noun → singular verb: The majority of the land is farmland.
  • The majority used alone (no of) can take a singular or plural verb (The majority was / were in favour).

Precision with data

  • the vast majority (≈ 80–99%), the majority / over half (> 50%), almost half, a third, a significant minority (≈ 20–40%), a small minority / a handful (< 10%), scarcely any / hardly any (≈ 0–3%).
  • Avoid vague a lot of / lots of in formal writing.
  • amount + uncountable; number + countable (not a large amount of people).

Concept-checking questions

  • The number of students who read daily has fallen. What has fallen: the students or the number? (The number.) So singular or plural verb? (Singular.)
  • A number of students have complained. Does a number of mean a specific figure? (No, it means several or quite a few.) So singular or plural verb? (Plural.)
  • Only a small minority of respondents read printed newspapers. Is this more or less than 50%? (Much less.) Would a significant proportion be correct for 4%? (No.)

Anticipated problems and solutions

ProblemSolution
The number of students have increased.Underline the head noun number; singular verb.
A large amount of people.Two columns: number (countable) / amount (uncountable).
Less students / fewer time.Rule: fewer + countable, less + uncountable; less with countable is common informally but avoid it in formal writing.
Imprecise expressions for figures.Map expressions onto a percentage scale on the board.
Overuse of a lot of in reports.Substitution task; highlight formal register.

CEFR C1 · 60 minutes · Teenagers and adults

Procedure: Formal Quantity Expressions and Agreement, step by step

Warm-up5 minutes · Whole class, pairs

Aim: To engage students and introduce the topic.

Quick class poll with a show of hands: "Who read a book for pleasure last month? Who reads news on a phone? Who reads a printed newspaper?" Board the numbers.

Pairs describe the results in one sentence each (2 minutes). Board two sentences as students say them (they will probably use a lot of / most / some).

Presentation20 minutes · Individual, groups, whole class

Aim: To present formal quantity expressions in a report and clarify their meaning, form and agreement.

Part A: Reading (6 minutes)

Give out Handout 1. Say: "Read the extract from a survey report on young people's reading habits. Complete the table with the percentages. Four minutes."

Pairs compare. Check as a class.

Part B: Analysis (6 minutes)

Give out Handout 2. Say: "In groups, find the quantity expressions. Put them on the scale. Then answer the questions about the verbs."

ICQs: "What do you put on the scale?" (The expressions.)

Monitor and help.

Part C: Clarification (8 minutes)

Feedback. Build the scale on the board.

Countable vs uncountable. Board a great deal of time vs a large number of students. Cross out a large amount of people.

Agreement. Board The number of students has fallen and A number of students have complained. Ask the CCQs.

the majority of. Contrast The majority of respondents were ... with The majority of the land is ...

Return to the Stage 1 sentences and rewrite them formally.

Controlled practice10 minutes · Individual, then pairs, then whole class

Aim: To practise the expressions and agreement accurately.

Give out Handout 3. Say: "Exercise A: choose the correct verb. Exercise B: choose the best expression for each figure. Exercise C: correct the errors. Seven minutes."

Pairs check. Check as a class.

Freer practice18 minutes · Pairs, then groups, then whole class

Aim: To use the expressions accurately in report writing.

Give out Handout 4. Pairs choose Survey A or Survey B and write a report paragraph (120–150 words) describing the key findings. They must use at least six expressions from today and get the figures right (10 minutes).

ICQs: "How many expressions?" (At least six.) "Can you write 'a lot of'?" (No.)

Peer review (5 minutes). Pairs swap paragraphs with a pair that wrote about the other survey. They check that each expression matches the figure and that the verbs agree.

Two pairs read their paragraphs to the class (3 minutes).

Monitor and note language use.

Feedback and wrap-up7 minutes · Whole class

Aim: To give feedback and consolidate.

Board sentences from the reports (correct and incorrect). Students correct the errors.

Ask: "What's the difference between 'a number of' and 'the number of'?"

Set the homework.

Homework Write a full report (250 words) on the other survey in Handout 4, with an introduction, findings and conclusion. Use at least eight formal quantity expressions and underline the subject and verb in each.

PPP (presentation, practice, production). Stage times add up to 60 minutes.

Differentiation

Support
Give weaker students the percentage scale on a card. Let them write sentence by sentence using the data table, not a full paragraph.
Stretch
Stronger students use hedged and comparative expressions: a marginally higher proportion, roughly two in five, a twofold increase, the figure stood at. They use inversion: Only a handful of respondents ...; Scarcely any ...

Adapting the lesson

ForWhat to change
A large class (40 or more)Keep the pairwork in "Freer practice" in fixed pairs rather than a mingle, and take feedback from four or five pairs instead of the whole room. Run "Peer review" once, with two pairs reporting, rather than hearing every pair. Monitor one half of the room during "Controlled practice" and the other half during "Freer practice".
A class with no photocopierPut the exercises from Handout 3: Practice on the board for learners to copy into their notebooks, and read any text aloud twice instead of printing it. The board plan above is this lesson's minimum - with that on the board, it runs on no paper at all.
An online classSend Handouts 1 to 4 in the chat before the lesson and put the board plan on a shared slide. Use breakout rooms for "Peer review" and visit each room once. Have learners type the controlled answers in the chat, so you see every answer at once instead of nominating.
A different age groupWith teenagers at this level, cut the academic framing and keep the examples in their own world; the forms do not change. With adults in a work context, swap the lesson's texts for ones from their own field and keep the analysis as it stands.

Checking learning

The report paragraph contains at least six formal quantity expressions that accurately match the figures. Subject–verb agreement is correct after the number of / a number of / the majority of. 10 out of 12 or better on Handout 3, Exercises A and B.

Teacher's reflection

Did students match the expressions to the figures precisely? Was the peer review useful for catching agreement errors?

Handout 1: Survey Report Extract

Name: ______________________________ Date: ______________

Reading Habits of Young People in Colombo: Key Findings

This survey is fictional and was written for this lesson.

A total of 500 respondents aged 16–24 took part in the survey. The vast majority of respondents (91%) own a smartphone, and over half (56%) said they read news online every day. By contrast, only a small minority (4%) still read a printed newspaper regularly, and scarcely any (2%) subscribe to a magazine.

The number of young people who read books for pleasure has fallen since the previous survey, from 48% to 35%. Nevertheless, a significant proportion of respondents (35%) said they had read at least one book in the past month, and a handful (6%) reported reading more than five.

A great deal of reading time is now spent on social media: respondents reported spending an average of three hours a day on it. A number of participants also commented that they had less time for reading because of study pressure. Finally, almost a third (31%) said they would read more if the local library opened in the evenings.

Handout 3: Practice

Name: ______________________________ Date: ______________

A. Choose the correct verb.

B. Choose the best expression for each figure.

C. Correct the errors.

  • The number of cars on the road has / have doubled.
  • A number of residents has / have complained about the noise.
  • The majority of the students was / were in favour of the change.
  • A great deal of money has / have been wasted.

Common errors to watch for

  • The subject is the number, which is singular. · 2. A number of participants have commented ... (plural, because it means several participants). · 3. Time is uncountable; a large number of is for countable nouns. · 4. No; time is uncountable, so less.
  • has · 2. have · 3. were · 4. has · 5. lives (singular with population; live is also possible in British English when the group is thought of as people) · 6. is
  • the vast majority · 2. scarcely any · 3. over half · 4. a handful · 5. a significant proportion · 6. almost half
  • A large number of people attended the meeting. · 2. The number of complaints has risen sharply. · 3. There were fewer applicants this year. · 4. A significant number / proportion of respondents said they were unhappy. (or the majority of, depending on the figure)
