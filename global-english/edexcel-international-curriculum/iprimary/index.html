Edexcel graded curriculum

Edexcel iPrimary English: Years 1 to 6

Six years of curriculum, three strands, and one external test at the end of it - taken on two texts the school has already had for a month.

The curriculum: three strands

The iPrimary English curriculum is organised into six year groups, each building on the last. It contains three main strands, each divided into sub-strands. The strand names below are Pearson's; the commentary on each is RCF English's own.

1. Spoken Language

Two sub-strands. This strand is not directly examined in the Year 6 test, which is written - but it underpins both of the others, and schools that treat it as optional tend to produce children who read accurately and write thinly.

  • Receptive Language - listening, following and understanding.
  • Expressive Language - speaking, explaining, discussing and presenting.

2. Reading

Six sub-strands, one of which stops after Year 3. This is the largest strand and it carries half of the marks in the Year 6 test.

  • Word Reading and Phonics (up to Year 3) - decoding, letter-sound relationships, blending.
  • Word Reading and Accuracy - reading unfamiliar words correctly and fluently.
  • Literal Comprehension - finding information that is actually stated.
  • Inferential Comprehension - working out what is implied but not said. This is where most marks are won and lost.
  • Text Structure and Purpose - how a text is organised, and why.
  • Grammar for Reading - using grammatical knowledge to understand meaning.

3. Writing

Six sub-strands. Note how much of this strand is technical accuracy rather than composition - which matches the test, where grammar, punctuation and spelling carry a quarter of the total mark on their own.

  • Transcription and Phonics - spelling and the sound-to-letter knowledge behind it.
  • Vocabulary - the range of words available and choosing between them.
  • Composition - planning, drafting and shaping a piece of writing.
  • Handwriting and Word Processing - legible handwriting and keyboard skills.
  • Grammar for Writing - sentence construction and control.
  • Punctuation - accuracy and its effect on meaning.

What those percentages tell you to teach

Grammar and punctuation is the single largest objective at 25% - larger than any reading objective and equal to the whole of writing. A quarter of this qualification rests on accuracy, and it is the most improvable thing on the list.

Inference (RAO2, 15%) is the largest reading objective, and it is three times the size of structure (RAO3, 5%). A child who can find stated facts but cannot say what a character is feeling will lose far more than one who cannot label a subheading.

Accurate writing (WAO3, 10%) outweighs task and audience (WAO1, 8%) and organisation (WAO2, 7%). In the 15-mark writing task, correctness matters more than ambition. A short, well-punctuated piece scores better than a long, exciting, error-strewn one.

Using the pre-release properly

The two Section A texts are published by Pearson at least four weeks before the test. Pearson's own guidance recommends that schools give the pre-release around two weeks of lesson time, eight to ten hours. The following sequence follows that guidance.

  1. Get the texts and read them yourself first

    Before any lesson. You cannot direct a child's attention to what matters in a text you met the same morning.

  2. Read them aloud, with expression

    Pearson recommends this explicitly. Children who have heard a text read well understand its tone, and tone is what RAO5 - 14% of the qualification - asks about.

  3. Work on vocabulary in context and in isolation

    Both. A child should know what a word means in the sentence where it appears and be able to say what it means on its own, because the test asks both ways.

  4. Interrogate the writer's language and purpose

    Why this word and not a plainer one? Who was this written for? What does the writer want the reader to feel? That is RAO4 and RAO5, 22% between them.

  5. Look at the non-fiction text for audience, purpose and presentation

    Headings, captions, images, layout - what each is doing and why it was put there.

  6. Make sure every child has their own copy

    To annotate, argue with and take home.

  7. Be clear about what happens on the day

    A clean Source Booklet containing both texts is supplied with the paper. Marked-up copies of the pre-release are not permitted in the examination room. Children who have relied on their annotations rather than on knowing the text can be badly caught out, so practise at least once with clean copies.

The practice material below is original RCF work

The activities that follow were written by RCF English. They are not Pearson materials, not past questions, and not sample assessment materials. They are written to rehearse the skills the assessment objectives name.

For official sample assessment materials and past papers, use the iPrimary English page on the Pearson website.

Original RCF practice: the four skills the test rewards most

Four short activities, each aimed at one of the heaviest assessment objectives. They can be used with any text, including the pre-release.

Preparing a Year 6 class

Do this

  • Download the pre-release the day it appears and plan two weeks around it.
  • Spend most of your reading time on inference, which is the largest reading objective.
  • Practise at least once with clean, unannotated copies of the texts.
  • Teach punctuation as something that changes meaning, not as a list of rules.
  • Time the writing task at 25 minutes so children learn what that feels like.

Avoid this

  • Treat the pre-release as optional or leave it to the last week.
  • Let children rely on annotations they will not be allowed to bring in.
  • Spend equal time on all five reading objectives - they are worth 8, 15, 5, 8 and 14 per cent.
  • Reward long writing. Fifteen marks does not need three pages.
  • Use a dictionary in practice tests. They are not allowed in the real one.
