Practical English

English for University Studies

At university, the first test of your English is not an essay: it is introducing yourself, asking a question in a lecture, and speaking up in a seminar. Seven speaking topics come first, each with a conversation you can hear and take a part in.

Start speakingSpeaking Practice
  1. Home
  2. Practical English
  3. English for University Studies

How to practise each topic

  1. Say the phrases

    Read each phrase aloud twice.

  2. Play the conversation

    Listen for the phrases in use.

  3. Take a part

    Press a name and speak that person's lines.

  4. Make it yours

    Change the details to match your own course.

1. Introducing yourself at orientation

In your first week you will introduce yourself dozens of times. Prepare two or three sentences: your name, your course, and one thing about you.

Say thisWhen
Hi, I'm Dilshan. I'm doing a degree in Computer Science.Introducing yourself
Which course are you on?Asking someone else
I'm from Matara, so this is my first time living away from home.Adding something about you
Are you in the same tutorial group?Finding classmates
Shall we swap numbers, in case one of us misses a lecture?Keeping in touch

The first morning

Two new students are waiting outside the lecture hall.

  1. DilshanHi, is this the room for the Introduction to Programming lecture?
  2. AnjaliI think so. I'm waiting for the same one. I'm Anjali, by the way.
  3. DilshanNice to meet you. I'm Dilshan. Which course are you on?
  4. AnjaliComputer Science. I'm from Jaffna. What about you?
  5. DilshanSame course! I'm from Matara. It's a bit strange being so far from home.
  6. AnjaliI know what you mean. Shall we swap numbers, in case one of us misses something?
  7. DilshanGood idea.

2. Asking questions in and after lectures

Many students never ask a question because they worry about their English. Lecturers are glad to be asked. A short, polite question is all you need.

Say thisWhen
Excuse me, could you repeat the last point, please?You missed something
Could you explain what you mean by variable?A word or idea is unclear
Sorry, could you speak a little more slowly?The lecture is too fast
Will these slides be available online?Asking about materials
Is this topic likely to be in the examination?Asking about assessment

After the lecture

A student waits to speak to the lecturer at the end of a lecture.

  1. StudentExcuse me, Dr Wijesinghe. Do you have a minute?
  2. Dr WijesingheOf course. What can I do for you?
  3. StudentI didn't quite understand the difference between a sample and a population. Could you explain it again?
  4. Dr WijesingheCertainly. The population is the whole group you are interested in, and the sample is the smaller group you actually study.
  5. StudentI see. So if we survey fifty students, they are the sample?
  6. Dr WijesingheExactly. Good question.
  7. StudentThank you. And will the slides be available online?
  8. Dr WijesingheYes, I'll put them up this evening.

3. Talking to your lecturer during office hours

Office hours are times when lecturers are available to help students. Go prepared: know exactly what you want to ask, and bring your notes or draft.

Say thisWhen
Good afternoon. Is this a good time?Arriving
I'm in your second-year Economics class.Saying who you are
I'm having trouble with...Explaining the problem
Would it be possible to have an extension?Asking for more time
Thank you, that's really helpful.Leaving

Asking for an extension

A student visits a lecturer during office hours.

  1. NadeeshaGood afternoon, Professor Silva. Is this a good time?
  2. Prof. SilvaYes, come in. How can I help?
  3. NadeeshaI'm Nadeesha, from your second-year Economics class. I've been ill this week, and I'm behind with the essay.
  4. Prof. SilvaI'm sorry to hear that. Are you feeling better now?
  5. NadeeshaYes, thank you. Would it be possible to have a few extra days?
  6. Prof. SilvaCan you bring a medical certificate? If so, you can submit it next Wednesday.
  7. NadeeshaYes, I have one. Thank you, that's really helpful.

4. Speaking up in seminars

In seminars, you are expected to speak. You do not need a perfect idea: agreeing with a reason, asking a question or adding an example all count.

PurposeSay this
Giving your viewIn my view, ... / It seems to me that...
Agreeing with a reasonI agree with Kasun, because...
Disagreeing politelyI see your point, but I'm not sure that...
Adding an exampleJust to add an example, in my village...
Asking for clarificationCould you explain what you mean by...?
Getting into the discussionCould I just come in here?

A seminar on social media

A tutor leads a small discussion group.

  1. TutorSo, does social media help students learn? Kasun, what do you think?
  2. KasunIn my view, it can help, because students share notes and ask each other questions in groups.
  3. FathimaCould I just come in here? I see your point, but I'm not sure it helps most students. It can also be very distracting.
  4. KasunThat's true. Maybe it depends on how it's used.
  5. TutorGood. Fathima, could you give an example?
  6. FathimaWell, I often start checking a study group and end up watching videos for an hour.

5. Group project meetings

Group projects succeed when everyone knows what to do and by when. Clear, polite English stops misunderstandings.

PurposeSay this
StartingShall we start? Let's decide who does what.
OfferingI can do the survey, if that's OK.
SuggestingHow about meeting again on Thursday?
CheckingSo, just to confirm, you're writing the introduction?
DeadlinesCan we all finish our parts by Monday?

Dividing the work

Three students plan a group presentation.

  1. RaviShall we start? We need to decide who does what for the presentation.
  2. HasiniI can do the survey and the charts, if that's OK.
  3. ImranGreat. I'll write the introduction and the conclusion.
  4. RaviThen I'll prepare the slides. Can we all finish our parts by Monday?
  5. HasiniMonday is fine. How about meeting on Tuesday to practise?
  6. ImranSo, just to confirm: survey and charts, Hasini; introduction and conclusion, me; slides, Ravi; practice on Tuesday.
  7. RaviPerfect.

6. Giving a presentation and answering questions

Tell the audience what is coming, deliver it clearly, and remind them what they heard. The hardest part for many students is the questions at the end: it is fine to take a moment to think.

StageSay this
OpeningGood morning. Today I'm going to talk about...
OutlineMy presentation has three parts. First..., then..., and finally...
Moving onThis brings me to my second point.
ConcludingTo sum up, the key message is...
A difficult questionThat's a good question. Let me think for a moment.
If you don't knowI'm not sure, but I'll find out and let you know.

Questions after a presentation

A student has just finished presenting a survey on how students travel to campus.

  1. HasiniTo sum up, most students travel by bus, but many would cycle if there were safe cycle lanes. Thank you for listening. Are there any questions?
  2. LecturerThank you, Hasini. How many students did you survey?
  3. HasiniWe surveyed eighty students from three faculties.
  4. LecturerAnd do you think the results would be the same at other universities?
  5. HasiniThat's a good question. Let me think for a moment. Probably not, because our campus is close to the town centre.

7. The library and university offices

Registering, borrowing books and sorting out problems all mean speaking to staff. Explain the problem clearly and have your student number ready.

Say thisWhen
I'd like to register for the second semester.Registration
Could you tell me where I can find books on statistics?In the library
How long can I borrow this for?Borrowing books
My student ID card isn't working.Reporting a problem
Here is my student number.Giving your details

At the library desk

A student needs help finding a book.

  1. StudentExcuse me, could you tell me where I can find books on statistics?
  2. LibrarianYes, they're on the second floor, in the mathematics section.
  3. StudentThank you. How long can I borrow a book for?
  4. LibrarianTwo weeks, and you can renew it online if nobody else has reserved it.
  5. StudentGreat. And my student card isn't working at the entrance gate.
  6. LibrarianLet me check. What's your student number?

8. Academic vocabulary, said correctly

These words appear in every subject. Learn the meaning, and practise the stress too: the stressed part is in capitals. A word said with the wrong stress can be hard for a lecturer to understand.

WordSay itMeaning
analyseA-na-lyseto examine something in detail
evidenceE-vi-dencefacts that show something is true
significantsig-NI-fi-cantlarge or important enough to notice
approacha-PPROACHa way of dealing with something
methodME-thoda planned way of doing something
hypothesishy-PO-the-sisan idea that a study tests
concludecon-CLUDEto decide after looking at the evidence
researchre-SEARCH or RE-searchcareful study to discover facts (both are heard)

9. Understanding assignment instructions

The first word of an assignment question tells you what to do. Students lose marks by describing when the question asks them to evaluate.

Instruction wordWhat you must do
DescribeSay what something is like, or what happened.
ExplainSay why or how something happens, with reasons.
CompareShow the similarities and the differences.
DiscussLook at different sides of an issue and reach a view.
EvaluateJudge the strengths and weaknesses, and conclude.
JustifyGive good reasons for a decision or opinion.

10. Academic writing in brief: style, paraphrasing and referencing

Your department will teach academic writing in detail. These four rules will keep you safe from the start.

The essentials

  • Formal style: no contractions (it is, not it's) and no informal words (children, not kids).
  • Careful claims: write this suggests, not this proves.
  • Paraphrase properly: understand the idea, cover the original, and write it in your own words and sentence structure.
  • Always reference: any idea or words from another writer need a reference, in the style your department asks for (such as APA or Harvard). Using them without one is plagiarism, even by accident.
