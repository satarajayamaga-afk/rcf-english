Practical English

English for University Studies

University English is not harder English: it is English used for particular jobs, such as following a lecture, reading a research article or writing an assignment. Ten topics that make the first semester much easier.

Start with academic vocabularyPractise essay writing
  1. Home
  2. Practical English
  3. English for University Studies

1. Academic vocabulary

A small group of words appears again and again in lectures, textbooks and assignments, whatever your subject. Learn these first.

WordMeaningExample
analyseto examine something in detailWe analysed the survey results.
evidencefacts that show something is trueThere is little evidence for this claim.
significantlarge or important enough to noticeThere was a significant increase in sales.
approacha way of dealing with somethingThis study uses a different approach.
factorone of the things that causes a resultCost is a major factor.
methoda planned way of doing somethingDescribe the method you used.
assumeto accept something as true without proofThe model assumes that prices stay the same.
concludeto decide after thinking about the evidenceThe authors conclude that more research is needed.

2. Understanding assignment instructions

The first word of an assignment question tells you what to do. Students lose marks by describing when the question asks them to evaluate.

Instruction wordWhat you must do
DescribeSay what something is like, or what happened. No judgement is needed.
ExplainSay why or how something happens, with reasons.
CompareShow the similarities and the differences.
AnalyseBreak a topic into parts and show how they are connected.
DiscussLook at different sides of an issue and reach a view.
EvaluateJudge the strengths and weaknesses, and give a conclusion.
JustifyGive good reasons for a decision or opinion.
OutlineGive the main points only, briefly.

3. Following lectures and taking notes

You cannot write everything a lecturer says. Listen for the signpost phrases that show what is important, and use short forms to write faster.

Signposts to listen for

  • The main point here is... : write this down.
  • There are three reasons... : number your notes 1, 2, 3.
  • For example... : a short example helps you remember the idea.
  • Moving on to... : a new topic is starting.
  • To sum up... : the most important points are coming again.

Short forms for faster notes

WriteMeaning
→leads to, causes
∴therefore
= / ≠is the same as / is different from
e.g. / i.e.for example / that is
b/c / w/ / w/obecause / with / without
↑ / ↓increase / decrease
imp.important

4. Reading academic texts efficiently

Academic texts are not read like novels, from the first word to the last. Read with a purpose, and decide quickly whether a text is useful.

A reading method

  1. Before you read, write down what you want to find out.
  2. Read the title, the abstract or introduction, and the conclusion first.
  3. Look at the headings, tables and diagrams.
  4. Now read the useful sections carefully, and make notes in your own words.
  5. Write down the author, year and title straight away, for your references.

5. Paraphrasing and summarising

Paraphrasing means writing an idea in your own words and sentence structure. Summarising means giving only the main points, in fewer words. Both still need a reference to the original author.

How to paraphrase

  1. Read the passage until you understand it fully.
  2. Cover it, and write the idea from memory in your own words.
  3. Compare with the original: change any phrases that are too close.
  4. Add the reference.

Example

Original: Students who sleep for fewer than six hours a night tend to perform worse in examinations than those who sleep for eight hours.

Too close: Students who sleep fewer than six hours tend to do worse in exams than students sleeping eight hours. (Only a few words have changed.)

A good paraphrase: Getting too little sleep appears to be linked with lower examination results. (Silva, 2022)

The example and the reference are invented for practice.

6. Referencing and avoiding plagiarism

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own, even by accident. Universities treat it very seriously. The solution is simple: whenever you use another person's idea, say where it came from.

The rules

  • Exact words go inside quotation marks, with a reference.
  • Paraphrased or summarised ideas also need a reference.
  • Common knowledge, such as Colombo is the commercial capital of Sri Lanka, needs no reference.
  • Every in-text reference must also appear in your reference list at the end.
  • Your university or department tells you which referencing style to use, such as APA or Harvard. Follow it exactly.

What an in-text reference often looks like

Recent research suggests that group study improves memory (Fernando, 2021).

The name and year are invented for this example. Check the exact format in your department's guide.

7. Writing in an academic style

Academic writing is formal, careful and precise. It avoids strong claims that cannot be proved.

Instead of thisWrite thisWhy
It's a big problem.It is a significant problem.No contractions; precise vocabulary
Lots of people think...Many researchers argue that...Specific and formal
This proves that...This suggests that...Careful, hedged claims
I think online learning is better.Online learning may offer several advantages.Less personal, more objective
KidsChildrenNo informal words
And also, ...Furthermore, ...Formal linking words

8. Emails to lecturers

A short, polite, clear email gets a faster reply. Use a clear subject line, a formal greeting, one purpose, and your name and course.

Example email

Subject: Question about Assignment 2 (BIO 101)

Dear Dr Perera,

I am a first-year student in your Introduction to Biology course. I have a question about Assignment 2. The instructions ask for 1,500 words: does this include the reference list?

Thank you for your time.

Kind regards,

Tharushi Jayawardena

Student number: 2026/BIO/0123

The names, course and number are invented for this example.

9. Giving a presentation

A good academic presentation tells the audience what is coming, delivers it clearly, and reminds them what they have heard.

StageUseful phrases
OpeningGood morning. Today I am going to talk about...
OutlineMy presentation has three parts. First..., then..., and finally...
Moving onNow let us look at... / This brings me to my second point.
Showing a slideAs you can see in this chart, ...
ConcludingTo sum up, ... / The key message is...
QuestionsThank you for listening. I would be happy to answer any questions.

10. Seminars and group discussions

In seminars you are expected to speak. You do not need a perfect idea: asking a good question or building on someone else's point also counts.

PurposeUseful phrases
Giving your viewIn my view, ... / It seems to me that...
AgreeingI agree with Nimal's point, because...
Disagreeing politelyI see what you mean, but I wonder whether...
Adding a pointBuilding on that, ... / I would also add that...
Asking for clarificationCould you explain what you mean by...?
Bringing others inWhat do you think, Anusha?
