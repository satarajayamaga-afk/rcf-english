IELTS Practice Test 1: Answers

Every answer, where it comes from, and why the wrong one was tempting.

  1. Home
  2. Practical English
  3. IELTS

Unofficial original RCF practice material

This key accompanies RCF IELTS Academic Practice Test 1, written by RCF English. It is not an official IELTS test or mark scheme and is not endorsed by the British Council, IDP: IELTS Australia or Cambridge University Press & Assessment.

The band conversion tables below are indicative only. IELTS sets the raw-score-to-band conversion separately for every live test, according to that test's difficulty, and never publishes it in advance. Treat any band you calculate here as a rough guide, not a prediction.

Listening: Part 1

Answers 1 to 10. Spelling must be correct. Alternatives separated by a slash are both accepted.
QAnswerWhere it comes from, and the trap
1Bramley"the surname is Bramley. B-R-A-M-L-E-Y". Spelled out, so there is no excuse for losing this one. Priya is the first name and is not asked for.
2retirement party"It's a retirement party". Trap: the examiner offers birthday and wedding reception in the question before it. Answers are never the words in the question.
314 / 14th / fourteenth"we've moved it to the fourteenth". Trap: the seventh of March is said first and then withdrawn. Whenever a speaker corrects themselves, the second version is the answer.
485 / eighty-five"I'd be surprised if more than eighty-five actually came". Trap: about a hundred were invited. The form asks how many are expected.
5Willow"the Willow Room would be the one". Trap: the Oak Room is named first, then ruled out because it is being redecorated.
632.50"thirty-two pounds fifty an hour at weekends". Trap: £28 is the weekday rate and the booking is a Saturday. The £ is already printed, so do not write it again.
7150"it's a hundred and fifty now". Trap: "It used to be a hundred pounds". Listen for the tense.
8tablecloths"The one thing we don't provide is tablecloths". Note the plural - tablecloth is marked wrong because it does not fit what was said.
940 / forty"There are forty spaces". The church overflow is extra information, not a number to write.
10Friday"please do that before Friday". Trap: this evening is what Priya says she will do, not what the centre asks for.

How to read the conversion tables below

IELTS does not publish a fixed raw-score-to-band table. Each live test is equated to the same standard, so a slightly harder test needs slightly fewer correct answers for the same band. The tables below are the approximate ranges that published practice materials have generally shown.

Use them to see roughly where you are and what you need to move up. Do not use them to predict a test-day score, and do not conclude from one practice test that you are ready to book.

Working out your overall band

Your overall band is the average of the four papers, rounded to the nearest half band. An average ending in .25 rounds up to the next half band; an average ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band.

So 6.5, 6.5, 6, 6 averages 6.25 and is reported as 6.5. But 6, 6, 6, 6.5 averages 6.125 and is reported as 6.

This is why the paper you are weakest at is usually the one worth practising, even though it is the one you least want to do.

Writing Task 1: model answer

Original RCF model answer (191 words)

Question: The table shows the number of visitors, in thousands, to four types of tourist attraction in one country in 2005, 2012 and 2019.

The table compares visitor numbers at four categories of tourist attraction in a single country at three points between 2005 and 2019.

Overall, museums and galleries were the most popular attraction throughout the period and also grew the fastest, while historic houses were the least visited and were the only category to decline. Theme parks changed very little.

Museums and galleries received 6.8 million visitors in 2005, rising to 9.1 million in 2012 and 12.4 million by 2019 - an increase of over 80 per cent across the period, and by 2019 they attracted more visitors than theme parks and national parks combined in 2005.

National parks showed the steepest proportional growth. Numbers climbed from 4.2 million to 5.6 million between 2005 and 2012, and then rose far more sharply to 8.3 million by 2019, almost doubling overall. As a result, national parks overtook theme parks between 2012 and 2019.

Theme parks, by contrast, were essentially static, peaking at 5.9 million in 2012 before falling back to 5.5 million, barely above their 2005 figure. Historic houses fell steadily throughout, from 3.1 million to 2.7 million and then to 2.2 million, a loss of roughly a third.

Why this answer works

  • There is an overview, and it is the second paragraph. This is the single most important structural feature of a Task 1 answer. Without a clear overview the Task Achievement mark is capped at Band 5, no matter how good the English is.
  • The overview names the highest, the fastest-growing, the lowest and the only faller - the four things a reader would want to know - and gives no figures. Figures belong in the body.
  • Data is grouped by pattern, not by row. The two risers are together, then the flat one and the faller. Working down the table row by row produces a list, not a description.
  • Comparisons are made, not just numbers listed: national parks overtook theme parks, more than theme parks and national parks combined in 2005. The instruction says make comparisons where relevant, and this is where the marks are.
  • Figures are converted where it helps - 12,400 thousand is written as 12.4 million - and proportions are given as well as raw numbers (over 80 per cent, almost doubling, roughly a third).
  • No reasons and no opinions. There is nothing here about why museums grew. Task 1 gives no marks for explanation and examiners treat it as off-task material.
  • 191 words. Comfortably over the 150 minimum without padding. Around 170 to 200 is the practical range.

Writing Task 2: model answer

Original RCF model answer (287 words)

Question: Some people believe that the most effective way to reduce crime is to give longer prison sentences. Others argue that there are better alternatives. Discuss both these views and give your own opinion.

Whenever crime rises, the demand for longer sentences follows, and it is easy to see why: the argument is simple, it sounds firm, and it offers something that can be done immediately. I understand its appeal, but I believe the evidence points towards a different approach.

The case for longer sentences rests on two claims. The first is deterrence: a person weighing up a crime will be put off by the prospect of ten years rather than three. The second is incapacitation, which is harder to dispute - somebody in prison cannot burgle a house. For a small number of persistently violent offenders, this is a genuine argument, and no serious alternative exists for them.

The difficulty is that deterrence appears to depend far more on the likelihood of being caught than on the length of the sentence. Someone committing a burglary is not calculating tariffs; they are assuming they will not be arrested at all. This explains why increasing police presence in an area tends to reduce crime quickly, while lengthening sentences produces little measurable effect. Long sentences also carry a cost that is rarely counted: prisoners lose work, housing and family contact, which are precisely the things that make reoffending less likely on release.

The alternatives are less satisfying politically because they are slow. Reliable investigation, drug and alcohol treatment, education inside prison, and supervised release with real support all reduce reoffending, but none of them can be announced as a single decisive measure.

In my view, prison should be reserved for those who present a danger to others, and the money saved by shorter sentences elsewhere should be spent on detection and rehabilitation. That is a less dramatic policy than simply doubling sentences, but I believe it would prevent considerably more crime.

Why this answer works

  • The question has two instructions and both are obeyed. Both views are discussed at real length, and the opinion is stated clearly - in the introduction and again in the conclusion. An essay that discusses both sides and never commits cannot reach Band 7 for Task Response.
  • The opposing view is given its strongest form, not a weak one. Somebody in prison cannot burgle a house is conceded outright, and one category of offender is granted to the other side. Conceding a real point is what distinguishes a Band 8 argument from a Band 6 one.
  • Every body paragraph has one idea and develops it. The third paragraph makes a single claim - that detection matters more than severity - and then explains the mechanism and adds a consequence. Examiners look for extension, not for more points.
  • Reasoning is used instead of invented evidence. There are no fabricated statistics and no studies have shown that 73 per cent.... Made-up figures do not earn marks and can look implausible.
  • Range shows in structure, not in long words. Whenever crime rises ... follows, The difficulty is that, which are precisely the things that - varied sentence forms used naturally. The vocabulary is precise (deterrence, incapacitation, tariffs, reoffending) but never decorative.
  • The conclusion adds a position, not a summary. It says what should happen and concedes the political weakness of its own recommendation, which reads as judgement rather than repetition.
  • 287 words. Over the 250 minimum with room to spare. Aim for 260 to 300: longer essays contain more errors, and accuracy is a quarter of the mark.

Speaking: model answers

Part 1 - short answers, extended by one step

Question: Do you enjoy cooking? / Do you think children should be taught to cook at school?

Do you enjoy cooking?

I do now, though I didn't for a long time. I think what changed it was cooking for other people - when it's just me I'll eat almost anything, but if someone's coming round I actually enjoy the planning part.

Do you think children should be taught to cook at school?

Yes, definitely, and I'd say it's more useful than some of the things they are taught. It's not really about the cooking either - it's that you learn to read instructions properly, and you learn that if you rush a stage, it shows. Those are quite general lessons, actually.

Why this answer works

  • Two or three sentences, not one, and not a lecture. Part 1 answers that are one word cost fluency marks; answers that run for a minute annoy the examiner and eat the time.
  • Each answer is extended by one step - a contrast (I didn't for a long time), a reason, or a consequence.
  • Natural speech features are present: actually, I'd say, quite. These are credited under fluency; over-formal written English is not.
  • The second answer reframes the question rather than accepting its terms, which produces more interesting language than agreeing.
Part 2 - the long turn

Question: Describe something practical that an older person taught you. You should say: who this person was, what they taught you, how they taught it to you, and explain why it has stayed with you.

I'm going to talk about my grandfather, who taught me how to sharpen a knife properly - which sounds like a very small thing, but I've thought about it more than almost anything else I was taught at that age.

He'd been a carpenter for about forty years, and he had this leather strop and an oilstone that he'd had since he was an apprentice. I must have been nine or ten. I remember being quite impatient about it, because I wanted to get on and use the knife, and he simply wouldn't let me.

The way he taught it was the interesting part. He didn't explain anything at first. He just did it, over and over, and made me watch - and then he handed me the stone and corrected the angle of my hand with his fingers, without saying very much at all. He said you couldn't learn it from being told, only from feeling when it was right. And he was correct: it took me about a week before something clicked and I could feel the difference.

As for why it's stayed with me - partly it's that I still do it, so there's a physical reminder every few months. But mainly I think it was the first time I understood that some things can't be rushed, and that there are skills you acquire in your hands rather than in your head. I've used that idea about all sorts of things since - learning an instrument, driving, even writing. So it turned out not to be about knives at all.

Why this answer works

  • All four bullet points are covered, in order. The card is a structure and using it removes any risk of drying up.
  • The fourth bullet gets the most time. Explain why is where the developed language lives, and weak candidates give it one sentence.
  • It lasts about two minutes at a natural pace, which is the top of the range. Stopping at forty seconds is the commonest Part 2 failure.
  • Narrative tenses are used naturally: He'd been a carpenter, I must have been nine, it took me about a week. Grammatical range is shown by telling a story properly, not by inserting complex structures.
  • It ends with a turn rather than trailing off - it turned out not to be about knives at all - which reads as control rather than as running out.
Part 3 - the discussion

Question: Why do some practical skills disappear within a single generation?

I think it usually happens when the skill stops being necessary rather than when people stop valuing it. My grandmother could mend clothes very well, and my mother can a bit, and I can't at all - but that's not because we respect it less. It's that a shirt costs almost nothing now, so the skill has no work to do.

The thing that makes it permanent, though, is that these skills are passed on by watching, not by reading. If one generation doesn't learn it, there's nobody left to demonstrate it, and a written description isn't enough - you can read how to sharpen a knife and still not be able to do it.

So I'd say there's a kind of tipping point. As long as a few people still do it, it can come back, and you see that with things like baking or repairing bicycles. But once it drops below that, it's effectively gone, and reviving it means starting again from books rather than from people.

Why this answer works

  • Part 3 answers are longer and more abstract than Part 1. Three developed points, roughly forty-five seconds.
  • A personal example supports a general claim rather than replacing it. The grandmother appears in one sentence and then the answer returns to the argument.
  • It connects back to Part 2 naturally - the knife reappears - which examiners regard as coherence, not repetition.
  • Hedged, precise language: I think it usually happens when, there's a kind of tipping point, effectively gone. Speculating carefully is exactly what Part 3 is designed to test.

What to do with your score

Do not just count the total. Go back to every question you got wrong and decide which of three things happened: you did not hear or find the information, you found it but misread the question, or you knew the answer and lost it on spelling, word limit or grammar. The third category is the easiest band to recover and most candidates have several.

Listening and Reading errors usually cluster by question type. If four of your six mistakes are on TRUE/FALSE/NOT GIVEN, that is one thing to fix, not six.

For Writing, mark yourself on each of the four criteria separately. Most candidates are a full band lower on one criterion than the other three, and that is where the work is.
