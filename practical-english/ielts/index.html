Practical English

IELTS

IELTS does not test how much English you know. It tests how well you use it under time pressure, in four fixed formats — which is a different skill, and one you can practise.

  1. Home
  2. Practical English
  3. IELTS

Who actually needs IELTS

In Sri Lanka most candidates sit IELTS for one of four reasons: study abroad, skilled migration, a professional registration overseas, or a visa requirement. Each of those wants a different score, and some want a minimum in every single module rather than an overall figure.

Find out your required score before you begin preparing. A candidate who needs 6.0 overall and a candidate who needs 7.0 in each band are preparing for two different examinations, and the second one usually needs months longer. Ask the university, the regulator or the visa authority directly — not an agent, and not a friend who sat it three years ago.

Academic or General Training?

Listening and Speaking are identical in both. Only Reading and Writing differ. Choosing the wrong one is a wasted fee.
AcademicGeneral Training
Usually taken forUniversity and professional registrationMigration, work, and some secondary or vocational study
ListeningThe same test in bothThe same test in both
ReadingThree long texts of the kind found in journals and serious newspapersShort workplace and everyday texts first, then one longer passage
Writing Task 1Describe a graph, chart, table, map or process in about 150 wordsWrite a letter — formal, semi-formal or personal — in about 150 words
Writing Task 2An essay of about 250 words. The same task type in both.An essay of about 250 words, usually on a slightly more everyday topic
SpeakingThe same test in bothThe same test in both

The four modules, and what each is really testing

Listening — about 30 minutes, 40 questions

Four recordings, moving from everyday conversation to an academic talk. You hear each one once.

What it is really testing: whether you can follow while continuing to read ahead. Most marks are lost not because the candidate failed to understand, but because they were still deciding on question 7 while the recording answered question 8.

Read the questions in the gaps. Underline what kind of answer each needs — a number, a name, a date, a plural noun. Knowing the shape of the answer before you hear it is most of the skill.

Spelling and grammar count. Wendesday is wrong. A plural written as a singular is wrong. If a question says no more than two words, three words scores zero however correct the meaning.

On paper you get transfer time at the end; on computer you do not. Know which format you are sitting, because it changes how you work.

Reading — 60 minutes, 40 questions

There is no extra transfer time. Write answers straight onto the answer sheet as you go.

What it is really testing: locating information quickly, not reading beautifully. You are not expected to understand every word — you are expected to find the part that answers the question and to read that part carefully.

Roughly twenty minutes per passage, and the third is usually the hardest. Candidates who read passage one thoroughly and run out of time on passage three lose more marks than they gained.

True / False / Not Given is where Sri Lankan candidates lose most. False means the text says the opposite. Not Given means the text does not say. If you are arguing from general knowledge or from what is probably true, the answer is Not Given.

Never leave a blank. There is no penalty for a wrong answer.

Writing — 60 minutes, two tasks

Task 1 about 150 words, Task 2 about 250 words. Task 2 carries roughly twice the weight of Task 1, so give it about forty of the sixty minutes.

Do Task 2 first if you are short of time — it is worth more. But do both: an unattempted task is a very heavy penalty.

Under the word count is penalised. Over it is not — but a 400-word essay usually contains more errors and worse organisation, so length is not a strategy.

Task 1 Academic is description, not opinion. Report what the figures show, select the significant features, compare. Do not explain why the trend happened and do not say whether it is good.

Task 1 General Training is a letter, and register decides the mark. Decide first whether it is formal, semi-formal or personal, then keep that register from Dear to the sign-off. Mixing them is the commonest error.

Task 2 must answer the question that was asked. A prepared essay on the general topic, however well written, scores badly if it does not address the specific instruction — discuss both views, to what extent do you agree, what are the causes and solutions.

Speaking — 11 to 14 minutes, three parts

Part 1: familiar questions about yourself, your home, your work or study. Four to five minutes.

Part 2: a card with a topic. One minute to prepare, then speak for one to two minutes without interruption. Use the preparation minute to write four or five words, not sentences.

Part 3: a discussion connected to the Part 2 topic, but more abstract. This is where higher bands are won or lost.

What it is really testing: fluency, range, accuracy and pronunciation — not opinions. There are no right answers and the examiner does not care whether they agree with you.

A Sri Lankan accent is not a problem. Pronunciation is marked on whether you are understood, not on whether you sound British or Australian. Trying to fake an accent usually makes candidates harder to understand, not easier.

Do not answer in one word, and do not recite. A memorised answer is recognised immediately and marked down. Extend naturally: give the answer, then one reason or one example.

The nine bands, in plain words

You receive a band for each module and an overall band. Half bands are used.
BandOfficial termWhat it means in practice
9Expert userFull command. Rare, and not required by most universities.
8Very good userOccasional slips only. Handles complex argument well.
7Good userGenerally accurate, occasional errors. A common professional-registration requirement.
6Competent userEffective command despite inaccuracies. A common undergraduate requirement.
5Modest userPartial command; copes with overall meaning in familiar situations.
4Limited userBasic competence in familiar situations only.
3Extremely limited userConveys and understands only general meaning in very familiar situations.
2Intermittent userNo real communication except the most basic information.
1Non-userNo ability beyond a few isolated words.
0Did not attemptNo answerable information provided.

How the overall band is worked out

Your four module scores are averaged, then rounded to the nearest whole or half band.

A score ending in .25 rounds up to the next half band; a score ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band. So 6.25 becomes 6.5, and 6.75 becomes 7.0. Anything lower rounds down: 6.125 becomes 6.0.

This is why one weak module is expensive. Three 7s and a 5.5 average to 6.625, which rounds to 6.5 — not 7. If you need 7 overall, the module you avoid practising is the one that decides your result.

Preparing sensibly

Do

  • Find out the exact score you need, in each module, before you start
  • Sit one complete timed test early, to find out where you actually are
  • Practise under the real time limit — untimed practice teaches the wrong habit
  • Learn the instructions by heart so you do not spend test time reading them
  • Write Task 2 essays by hand if you are sitting the paper test
  • Read something in English every day that you did not choose for the exam
  • Book the test only when your practice scores are consistently at or above your target

Avoid

  • Memorising model answers — examiners identify them quickly and mark them down
  • Learning long "impressive" words you cannot use accurately; a wrong big word costs more than a right small one
  • Practising only your strongest module because it feels good
  • Treating IELTS as a general English course — it is a test-format skill
  • Booking the test to "see what happens", which is an expensive way to find out
  • Believing anyone who guarantees a band score

The mistakes we see most often

Preparing for the wrong test. Candidates who need General Training practising Academic Task 1 graphs for weeks. Check which one your purpose requires, and check it in writing.

Ignoring the word count. Under 150 or under 250 words is penalised every time, and under pressure most candidates write less than they think. Learn what 250 of your own words looks like on the page.

Answering the topic instead of the question in Task 2. The topic might be technology; the question might be do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages. Those need different essays.

Silence in Speaking Part 2. One minute of preparation feels short, so candidates start speaking without a plan and stop after forty seconds. Four words on the paper is enough to keep going for two minutes.

Treating Not Given as False in Reading. They are different answers and mixing them can cost several bands' worth of marks across a paper.

Where to get official practice material

We do not host IELTS practice tests here, and we will not. The IELTS papers in circulation online are almost all Cambridge copyright, and copying them would be theft from the people who wrote them — whatever the site you found them on.

The test owners publish genuine free practice material themselves. Use it in preference to anything else, because it is the only material written to the real specification:

Official IELTS preparation resources and official sample test questions at ielts.org.

Booking in Sri Lanka: IELTS is administered here by IDP and the British Council. IDP's Sri Lanka page is at ielts.idp.com/srilanka. For the British Council, search for their Sri Lanka examinations page directly — we have not linked it because we could not verify the exact address at the time of writing.

Confirm fees, test dates, formats and centre locations on the official sites. Those change, and anything we printed here would be out of date before you read it.

What RCF English offers, honestly

This page is guidance on the test and how to approach it, written from experience of what Sri Lankan candidates get wrong. It is free and it is ours.

We do not currently publish IELTS practice papers, model answers or graded writing feedback on this site. If we add them they will be written by RCF English, not copied from a coursebook.

The general English work in Practical English — grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation and the four skills — is useful preparation, because a candidate whose English improves scores better than one who only learns test tricks.
