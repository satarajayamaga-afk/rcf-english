Describing Graphs and Charts

Most candidates already know the words rise and fall. What separates a 6 from a 7 is knowing which grammar each one takes, and choosing what not to mention.

  1. Home
  2. Practical English
  3. IELTS
  4. Describing Graphs and Charts

About the figures on this page

All the data below is invented by RCF English for practice. It is not real, it describes no real country or organisation, and it must not be quoted as fact.

Its only purpose is to give you something accurate to write about. Real Task 1 questions use real data, but the language you need is identical.

The mistake that caps most answers at Band 5

Writing down every number in the order it appears.

Task 1 asks you to select the significant features and make comparisons. A list of all twenty figures shows you can read a chart; it does not show you can write. The examiner is looking for an overview — the two or three things a person would notice from across the room — and you are marked down if it is missing.

Select. You are not required to mention every figure, and you should not.

Verbs and nouns of change — and the grammar each takes

Written by RCF English. The right-hand column is where marks are actually lost.
Verb formNoun formHow it is used
rise / rose / risena riseSales rose. — no object. Never sales rose the profit.
increasean increaseNumbers increased to 500 (endpoint) · increased by 200 (amount).
grow / grewgrowthgrowth is uncountable — a rapid growth is wrong; write rapid growth.
fall / fell / fallena fallPrices fell to 40. Never fell down.
declinea declineSlightly formal; good for gradual movement.
decreasea decreasedecreased from 90 to 60.
dropa dropSuggests suddenness. A sharp drop.
plummet—A very steep fall. Use once at most, or it sounds rehearsed.
fluctuatea fluctuationUp and down with no clear direction.
remain stable / level off / plateau—Numbers levelled off at 200 — stopped changing after a period of change.
peaka peakSales peaked at 500 in 2018 — the highest point, with the year.
reach a low ofa lowFigures reached a low of 12.
overtake—Mobile phones overtook televisions in 2014 — passed another category.
double / triple—The figure doubled — no by. Doubled by two is wrong.

Saying how much, and how fast

Pair one adjective with a noun, or one adverb with a verb. Both say the same thing — use each shape at least once.
DegreeAdjective + nounVerb + adverb
Very largea dramatic rise · a sharp increaserose dramatically · increased sharply
Largea significant increase · a considerable fallincreased significantly · fell considerably
Moderatea steady rise · a gradual declinerose steadily · declined gradually
Smalla slight fall · a marginal increasefell slightly · increased marginally
Very smalla negligible changebarely changed
Fasta rapid increaseincreased rapidly
Slowa slow declinedeclined slowly

Prepositions — the commonest errors we see

These four lines cost more marks than any vocabulary gap.
Write thisNot thisWhy
rose by 30rose with 30by = the size of the change. with is a first-language habit.
rose to 500rose until 500to = the endpoint reached.
from 2010 to 2020 · between 2010 and 2020from 2010 until 2020 · between 2010 to 2020from...to and between...and are fixed pairs. Do not mix them.
in 2015 · at 40% · at its peakon 2015 · in 40%Years take in; values take at.
a rise of 20%a rise of 20% points (unless it truly is percentage points)A rise of an amount; a rise to a level.

The structure, in four paragraphs

1. Introduction — one sentence. Paraphrase the question. Change the wording, not the meaning. Copied introductions are not counted in your word total.

**2. Overview — two sentences, signalled with Overall. The biggest thing, and the second biggest. No figures needed here. This paragraph earns the most and is the one candidates leave out.**

3. Detail paragraph one. One group of the data, with figures as support.

4. Detail paragraph two. The rest, with comparison.

About 170 to 190 words. Under 150 is penalised.

Practice 1 — a line graph, given as a table

The task: The table below shows the number of visitors, in thousands, arriving from three regions between 2015 and 2020. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. Write at least 150 words.

Sketch it as a line graph first if that helps — three lines, six points each.

Visitors by region, in thousands (practice data)

Invented by RCF English for practice. Not real statistics.
YearEuropeAsiaThe Americas
201512020040
201613524042
201715030045
201816535547
201917038050
2020459012

Model answer 1, with the reasoning shown

Read the model answer (183 words)

The table shows how many visitors, in thousands, came from Europe, Asia and the Americas over the six years from 2015 to 2020.

Overall, arrivals from all three regions grew steadily until 2019 and then collapsed in 2020. Asia was the largest source throughout the period and also showed by far the greatest growth.

Asian arrivals rose from 200,000 in 2015 to 380,000 in 2019, an increase of 90 per cent and the steepest climb of the three. European numbers grew more modestly over the same years, from 120,000 to 170,000, while arrivals from the Americas increased only marginally, from 40,000 to 50,000.

In 2020 the pattern reversed sharply. Asian arrivals fell to 90,000, less than a quarter of the previous year's figure, and European visitors dropped to 45,000. The Americas, which had always contributed fewest, fell to 12,000. Despite this fall, the ranking of the three regions was unchanged: Asia remained the largest source and the Americas the smallest in every year shown.

Why it would score well

The overview is separate and signalled. Overall tells the examiner exactly where to find it. Two features only: growth then collapse, and Asia dominant.

It selects. Eighteen figures are available; twelve are used. The others add nothing.

It compares rather than lists. by far the greatest growth, more modestly, only marginally, always contributed fewest.

It varies the grammar. rose from... to, an increase of 90 per cent, grew more modestly, dropped to, fell to — both the verb+adverb and adjective+noun shapes appear.

It ends with a comparison, not a new fact. The ranking sentence pulls the whole thing together.

Note what is absent: no explanation of why 2020 fell. Task 1 never asks for reasons, and speculating wastes words you need elsewhere.

Practice 2 — a bar chart, given as a table

The task: The chart below shows the percentage of households owning selected items in one country in 2005 and 2020. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Households owning each item, per cent (practice data)

Invented by RCF English for practice. Not real statistics.
Item20052020
Mobile phone2595
Television6088
Refrigerator3072
Computer845
Car514

Model answer 2, with the reasoning shown

Read the model answer (176 words)

The chart compares the proportion of households owning five different items in 2005 and in 2020.

Overall, ownership of every item rose over the fifteen years. The most striking change was in mobile phones, which went from the third most common item to the most common of all, while cars remained by far the least widely owned in both years.

In 2005 the television was the item most households possessed, at 60 per cent, followed by the refrigerator at 30 per cent and the mobile phone at 25 per cent. By 2020 this order had changed completely: 95 per cent of households owned a mobile phone, overtaking the television, which had itself risen to 88 per cent.

The refrigerator and the computer both more than doubled, reaching 72 and 45 per cent respectively, with the computer showing the largest proportional increase of any item, from a very low base of 8 per cent. Car ownership grew least in absolute terms, from 5 to 14 per cent, and remained a minority in 2020.

Why it would score well

It finds the story, not just the numbers. The story is the mobile phone overtaking the television — a reader would notice that first, so it belongs in the overview.

**It distinguishes two kinds of biggest.** The mobile phone rose most in percentage points (70); the computer rose most proportionally (nearly six times over). Noticing that difference is a Band 7 move.

**It uses respectively correctly** — two items named in one order, two figures given in the same order. Used wrongly it is worse than not used at all.

It groups. Items are handled in sensible pairs, not one sentence each in chart order.

It closes the comparison it opened. Cars are mentioned in the overview and again at the end.

Three more practice tasks — no model answers, so you have to decide

Practice 3 — two pie charts (household spending)

The task: The charts show how an average household divided its monthly spending in 2000 and in 2020. Practice data, invented by RCF English:

2000: Food 45% · Housing 20% · Other 17% · Transport 10% · Education 8%

2020: Food 30% · Housing 28% · Transport 15% · Education 12% · Other 15%

Before you write, decide: which category changed most, and does most mean percentage points or proportion? Which is still the largest? Which overtook which? Your overview should answer the first and last of those.

Pie-chart language: accounted for, made up, a share of, the largest proportion, by contrast. Percentages in each chart must total 100 — check before you claim a trend.

Practice 4 — a process diagram (no numbers)

The task: The diagram shows how black tea is produced. Stages: plucking → withering → rolling → oxidation → drying → grading → packing.

A process needs different language from a graph. There is no overview of trends; instead say how many stages there are and where the process begins and ends.

Use the passive: The leaves are withered for several hours, not workers wither the leaves. The agent is rarely important.

Sequence carefully: First · Next · Once this has been done · At this stage · Finally. Do not write Firstly, secondly, thirdly down the whole answer.

No figures, no comparisons, no opinion. Candidates who have only practised graphs often panic here — the structure is simpler, not harder.

Practice 5 — two maps (a town centre, then and now)

The task: The maps show a town centre in 1990 and today. Changes: the factory by the river became apartments; the car park became a park; a footbridge was added; the primary school doubled in size; the row of small shops became a supermarket.

Maps need location and change language together. to the north of · on the opposite bank · adjacent to · in the north-east corner · where the factory once stood.

Use the passive for change: The factory was demolished and replaced by apartment blocks. · A footbridge was constructed across the river. · The school was extended.

The overview should say how much changed overall — for example that the area became more residential and less industrial. That single sentence is worth more than any individual detail.

Checking your own answer

Do

  • Write an overview and start it with Overall
  • Give the years or units in the introduction
  • Support each claim with one figure, not five
  • Compare — the word while or whereas should appear at least twice
  • Use both grammar shapes: rose sharply and a sharp rise
  • Count your words once, early, so you know what 170 looks like in your handwriting

Avoid

  • Explaining why a trend happened — never asked, never credited
  • Saying whether a trend is good, worrying, or encouraging
  • Copying the question wording into your introduction
  • Listing every figure in chart order
  • Increased with, rose until 500, between 2010 to 2020
  • Ending with a conclusion that repeats the overview — Task 1 needs no conclusion

Compare your writing against official marked answers

Once you have written two or three of the tasks above, read the test owner's official example responses with band scores and examiner comments (PDF, ielts.org).

Real candidate answers, marked, with the examiner explaining the band. Reading why one answer got 6 and another 7 will teach you more than any amount of advice — including this page.
