Mixed-Ability Teaching

Every English class is a mixed-ability class. The question is only whether the teaching acknowledges it.

  1. Home
  2. English Teachers Resources
  3. Mixed-Ability Teaching

The impossible situation, stated honestly

Forty students. Six of them could sit the examination today. Eight cannot write a correct simple sentence. The rest are spread between. One teacher, forty minutes, and a syllabus.

There is no arrangement that serves all forty perfectly. What follows is not a solution but a set of practical compromises that work better than teaching to the middle and hoping.

Five strategies that work

  1. Open tasks instead of graded worksheets

    Write about your journey to school can be answered in three sentences or thirty. Everyone succeeds at their own level, in one task, with no preparation of multiple versions. This is the single most useful technique in a mixed class.

  2. One task, tiered support

    The same question for everyone, with support available: sentence starters on the board, a word bank, a worked example. Students take what they need, and nobody is publicly assigned to a level.

  3. Extension built in, not improvised

    Two harder questions on the board from the start. Without them, the fastest six students are unoccupied for ten minutes of every lesson — which is also where most classroom disruption begins.

  4. Deliberate pairing

    Not friendship pairs and not random pairs. Pair a strong student with a middling one, and a middling one with a weaker one. A very strong student paired with a very weak one usually helps neither.

  5. Targeted questioning

    Decide before the lesson which question goes to which student. Give the accessible question to the student who needs a success, and the demanding one to the student who needs stretching. Done naturally, the class does not notice, and every student is asked something they can engage with.

Making one task work at three levels

TaskFor those who need supportFor those who need stretching
Write a noticeFive headings supplied down the pageAdd: write a second notice cancelling the first
Comprehension passageParagraph number given for each questionAdd: what does the writer think, and how do you know?
Write a paragraphTopic sentence suppliedAdd: rewrite it from the opposite point of view
Grammar exerciseFirst three items completedAdd: write three items of your own for a partner
Describe a pictureTen words listed on the boardAdd: what happened just before this moment?
Literature: a characterThree quotations suppliedAdd: which of these three is most revealing, and why?

Grouping: when, and when not

  • Mixed groups for discussion, activities and projects. Weaker students hear better English; stronger students consolidate by explaining.
  • Similar-ability groups for short, focused practice on a specific weakness — and only briefly. Permanent ability groups become permanent identities, and students placed in the bottom one stop trying.
  • Pairs for most things, in a large class. No movement, no noise, and twice as much speaking as any group activity.
  • Change the groups regularly. Whatever system you use, if it never changes it becomes a hierarchy the students believe in.

One realistic ambition per lesson

You cannot move forty students forward at their own pace every lesson. What you can do is decide, before each lesson, which group this lesson is genuinely aimed at — and make sure that over a fortnight each group has had its turn.

That is achievable, and it is far better than the alternative, which is aiming permanently at the middle and losing both ends.
