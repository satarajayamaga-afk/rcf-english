# Present Simple and Present Continuous for Past Events (Historic Present)

*Lesson theme: Telling It Live: The Present for Stories, Commentary and Headlines*

## Lesson overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Grammar topic** | Present Tenses |
| **Grammar point** | Present Simple and Present Continuous for Past Events (Historic Present) |
| **Level** | C1 (Advanced) |
| **Duration** | 60 minutes |
| **Framework** | TBL (Jane Willis): Pre-task, Task cycle (Task, Planning, Report), Language focus (Analysis, Practice). Meaning and communication come first and the grammar focus comes from the task. |
| **Class profile** | Advanced students with a strong command of tenses. They tell stories in the past tense only and are not aware of how and why present forms are used for dramatic effect, live commentary, headlines and summaries. |
| **Main aim** | To develop students' ability to recognise and use present forms for dramatic narrative (the historic present), live commentary, newspaper headlines and plot summaries. |
| **Subsidiary aims** | To develop fluency and vividness in spoken anecdotes; to raise awareness of genre conventions. |
| **Learning outcome** | By the end of the lesson, students can tell a short anecdote in the historic present, deliver a 60-second live commentary and write headlines and a plot summary using appropriate present forms. |
| **Materials** | Handout 1 (pre-task headlines), Handout 2 (task instructions and story prompts), Handout 3 (texts for language analysis), Handout 4 (practice), phones for recording (optional), a short silent video clip (sport or cooking, 60–90 seconds) or a set of sequenced images. |

## Language analysis

### Uses of present forms with past or general reference

| Genre | Typical forms | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Spoken anecdote (historic present) | Present continuous for background; present simple for main events | *So I'm walking home and this man comes up to me and says ...* |
| Live commentary | Present simple for quick completed actions; continuous for longer ones | *He passes to Kumar, Kumar shoots ... and it's in! The crowd is going wild.* |
| Newspaper headlines | Present simple for past events; *to* + infinitive for future; past participle for passive | *Minister resigns over scandal · President to visit Jaffna · Three injured in crash* |
| Plot summaries, reviews and reporting what writers argue | Present simple | *In the novel, the hero returns home ... · Smith argues that ...* |
| Jokes | Present simple / continuous | *A man walks into a bar ...* |

### Why use the present?
- It creates **immediacy and drama**: the listener feels the action is happening now.
- Speakers often **switch** from past to present at the most exciting moment of a story.
- Headlines use the present to save space and to make news feel current.
- Summaries use the present because the story "exists" in the text permanently.

### Discourse features of anecdotes
- Fillers and markers: *So ..., Anyway ..., And then ..., Next thing I know ..., Suddenly ...*
- Reporting with *says* / *goes* (informal): *And he goes, "Excuse me, is this your bag?"*

### Pronunciation
- Commentary: fast pace, rising pitch at climaxes, stress on names and key verbs.
- Anecdotes: pausing before the key moment for effect.

## Anticipated problems and solutions

| Problem | Solution |
|---|---|
| Students mix tenses randomly rather than for effect. | In analysis, highlight where switches happen and why. Rule of thumb: start in the past, switch to the present for the drama, and stay there. |
| Students don't see the historic present as "correct" English. | Show authentic-style examples from speech, journalism and literature. Discuss register: very common in speech, less common in formal written narrative. |
| Over-use of *goes* for *says* in formal contexts. | Point out it is informal speech only. |
| Weak students struggle to produce commentary in real time. | Let them watch the clip once first and note verbs; allow pairs to share the commentary. |
| No video available. | Use a sequence of six images (e.g. a cricket shot or a cooking process) displayed one by one. |

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Pre-task (8 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To introduce the topic, activate schemata and set up the task.
**Interaction:** Whole class, then pairs

1. Give out Handout 1. Say: *"Here are six newspaper headlines. With a partner, guess the full story behind each one."* (3 minutes.)
2. Take some guesses. Ask: *"Did these events happen in the past or are they happening now?"* (Past.) *"So why are the verbs in the present?"* Do not explain yet; say *"We'll come back to that."*
3. Tell a short anecdote yourself (about 1 minute), switching into the historic present at the dramatic moment. For example: *"Last week I was at the market. It was busy. So I'm standing at the fish stall, and this man comes up behind me and grabs my bag ..."*
4. Ask: *"Did you notice anything about how I told the story?"* Accept ideas.
5. **Set the task.** Say: *"Today you're going to be storytellers and commentators. First, tell a partner a true or invented story, and make it as dramatic as possible. Then you will do a live commentary."* Give out Handout 2.

### Stage 2: Task (15 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To give students the opportunity to use language freely for a real communicative purpose.
**Interaction:** Pairs, then groups of four

1. **Part A, anecdote (7 minutes).** Each student chooses a prompt from Handout 2 (a lost item, a surprise, an embarrassing moment, a near miss). They have one minute to think, then tell their story to a partner in about two minutes. The partner listens and asks two questions.
   - ICQs: *"How long is your story?"* (About two minutes.) *"What does your partner do?"* (Listens and asks two questions.)
2. **Part B, commentary (8 minutes).** Play the silent video (or show the images). Say: *"Watch once. Now, in your group of four, take turns to give a 60-second live commentary while the video plays again. Record it on your phone if you can."*
3. Monitor throughout without correcting. Note language used (or avoided) for later analysis.

### Stage 3: Planning (7 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To give students time to prepare a polished version for the class, focusing on accuracy and impact.
**Interaction:** Groups

1. Say: *"Each group chooses the best story and the best commentary. Help the speakers make them even more dramatic and accurate. You will present to the class."*
2. Groups improve the story and commentary. They may write key sentences. Help with language on request but do not teach the grammar point yet.

### Stage 4: Report (8 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To let groups share their work and to provide a purpose for listening.
**Interaction:** Whole class

1. Each group presents one story or one commentary (about 1 minute each).
2. Listening task for the class: *"Which story was the most dramatic? Why?"*
3. Board two or three sentences you heard that used present forms effectively, and one that did not.
4. Ask: *"When did the speakers switch to the present? What was the effect?"*

### Stage 5: Language focus, analysis (12 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To raise awareness of the forms and functions of the present in the different genres.
**Interaction:** Groups, then whole class

1. Give out Handout 3 (four extracts: anecdote, commentary, headlines, plot summary). Groups answer the analysis questions (6 minutes).
2. Feedback as a class. Build the centre of the board: which forms appear in each genre, and why.
3. Discuss:
   - Where does the speaker switch from past to present in Extract 1? Why there?
   - What do the headlines leave out? (Articles, auxiliaries.)
   - Why is the plot summary in the present?
4. Highlight the anecdote language (right of the board) and its informal register.

### Stage 6: Language focus, practice (10 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To practise the forms accurately.
**Interaction:** Individual, then pairs

1. Students complete Handout 4, Exercises A and B (6 minutes).
2. Pairs compare. Check answers as a class.
3. Exercise C (headline writing): pairs write three headlines for stories told in the lesson and read them out. The class guesses which story each refers to.

## Differentiation

**Support**
- Provide the anecdote language as a written list during the task.
- Let weaker students tell their story first in the past, then retell it in the present with a partner's help.

**Stretch**
- Stronger students compare the historic present with free indirect speech in a short literary extract.
- They write a plot summary of a film using the present simple and at least two participle clauses.

## Assessment and success criteria
- Students switch to the present deliberately at the climax of their story.
- The commentary uses the present simple for quick actions and the continuous for longer ones.
- Students can explain why headlines and summaries use the present.
- 8 out of 10 or better on Handout 4.

## Homework
Write a 200-word synopsis of a film or book using the present tense, and three headlines about real events from this week.

## Teacher's reflection
- Did the task generate a natural need for the target language?
- Did students use the present in the Report stage more effectively than in the first Task?
- Was the analysis stage long enough?

# Answer Key

**Handout 1:** The headlines use the present simple for past events (1, 2, 4, 5), *to* + infinitive for a future plan (3) and *set to* for a likely future (6). The present makes the news feel current and saves space.

**Handout 3**
1. Past: was, was flying, was. The switch happens at *So I'm sitting there*, just before the dramatic event, to make it feel immediate.
2. *I'm sitting, I'm reading, are screaming, are flying*: background and ongoing actions.
3. They are quick, completed actions happening as the commentator speaks.
4. Articles and auxiliaries are missing (*A storm has hit the coast; The new bridge is to open; A painting has been stolen*). *To open* = will open (future plan). *Stolen* = passive.
5. Plot summaries conventionally use the present because the story exists permanently in the text.
6. Extract 1: *So, like, totally, goes*.

**Handout 4, Exercise A (model)**
So a car stops in front of me. The driver opens the window and asks me for directions. I point to the left, and he smiles and drives away. Then I realise he has my bag!

**Handout 4, Exercise B**
1. President visits flood area · 2. Government to build new airport · 3. Two hurt in fire · 4. In the film, a young girl finds a lost dog and looks for its owner. · 5. He hits the ball and it goes for six! · 6. So I'm walking home and this woman stops me.

**Common errors to watch for**

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| (Plot summary) At the end, Hamlet died. | At the end, Hamlet dies. |
| Mixing tenses randomly within one scene. | Switch tense once, at the dramatic moment, and stay in the present. |
| Prime Minister is resigning today (headline). | Prime Minister resigns / Prime Minister to resign. |
| Using *goes* for *says* in a formal essay. | Use *says* or a reporting verb in formal writing. |
