# Past Perfect in Inversion and Participle Clauses (No sooner had ... / Having + past participle)

*Lesson theme: Crafting Suspense: Writing a Story Opening*

## Lesson overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Grammar topic** | Past Tenses |
| **Grammar point** | Past Perfect in Inversion and Participle Clauses (No sooner had ... / Having + past participle) |
| **Level** | C1 (Advanced) |
| **Duration** | 60 minutes |
| **Framework** | TBL: Pre-task, Task cycle (Task, Planning, Report), Language focus (Analysis, Practice). |
| **Class profile** | Advanced students who write accurate but "flat" narratives in which every sentence has the same structure. They have met negative inversion but rarely use it. |
| **Main aim** | To develop students' ability to create suspense in written narrative using delaying structures, negative inversion, *was about to / was going to*, and participle clauses. |
| **Subsidiary aims** | To develop collaborative writing and peer-editing skills; to raise awareness of sentence rhythm. |
| **Learning outcome** | By the end of the lesson, students can rewrite a flat paragraph into a suspenseful one using at least four techniques, and explain the effect of each. |
| **Materials** | Handout 1 (two openings), Handout 2 (task: the flat paragraph), Handout 3 (analysis), Handout 4 (practice and style checklist). |

## Language analysis

### Techniques

| Technique | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Delaying the main event | past continuous + *when* | I **was just reaching** for the door **when** the light went out. |
| Negative inversion | *No sooner had + S + pp ... than*; *Hardly / Scarcely / Barely had + S + pp ... when* | **No sooner had I sat down than** the phone rang. **Hardly had** she left **when** the storm began. |
| Inversion with *Never / Not until / Only then* | adverbial + auxiliary + subject | **Never had I felt** so alone. **Only then did I realise** the truth. |
| Interrupted plans | *was about to / was going to* + infinitive | I **was about to** scream **when** a hand covered my mouth. |
| Participle clauses | *Having + pp*, *-ing* clause | **Having locked** the door, she turned off the lights. **Hearing** a noise, he froze. |
| Sentence rhythm | long sentence followed by a very short one | ... and I walked slowly down the corridor, counting the doors, listening for any sound. **Silence.** |

### Accuracy points
- *No sooner* ... **than** (not *when*); *Hardly / Scarcely* ... **when** (not *than*).
- The participle clause must share the subject of the main clause (avoid dangling participles: *Having locked the door, the lights went out* ✗).

### Pronunciation (when reading aloud)
- Pause before the key event; lower volume for tension; stress the inverted auxiliary: *No sooner HAD I ...*

## Anticipated problems and solutions

| Problem | Solution |
|---|---|
| Wrong word order after inversion (*No sooner I had arrived*). | Show the "question order" pattern; quick transformation drill. |
| *No sooner ... when* / *Hardly ... than*. | Board the pairs clearly and include in practice. |
| Dangling participles. | Include one deliberate example in the analysis for students to spot. |
| Overuse of techniques making writing heavy. | Discuss balance in feedback; checklist limits each technique to once or twice. |

## Procedure

### Stage 1: Pre-task (8 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To introduce the topic of suspense and set up the task.
**Interaction:** Whole class, then pairs

1. Read aloud two openings from Handout 1 (Version A: flat; Version B: suspenseful). Ask: *"Which one would you continue reading? Why?"*
2. Pairs discuss what makes Version B more exciting (2 minutes). Take ideas but do not name the grammar yet.
3. **Set the task.** Give out Handout 2. Say: *"Your task is to rewrite this flat paragraph so that it is full of suspense. Then you'll read it aloud to the class and we'll vote for the most gripping version. Work in pairs."*

### Stage 2: Task (15 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To give students a meaningful writing task in which they use their existing language.
**Interaction:** Pairs

1. Pairs rewrite the paragraph (10 minutes). They may add details, change the order and change sentence lengths, but the basic events must stay the same.
2. Monitor without correcting. Note structures students use or avoid.
3. After 10 minutes, pairs swap with another pair and read each other's versions (3 minutes). Each pair underlines the line they think is most effective.

### Stage 3: Planning (7 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To prepare for public performance and improve the text.
**Interaction:** Pairs

1. Say: *"You will read your version to the class. Improve it using your partners' feedback. Practise reading it aloud: where will you pause?"*
2. Help with language on request.

### Stage 4: Report (8 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To share the outcomes and provide a reason to listen.
**Interaction:** Whole class

1. Four or five pairs read their versions aloud (about 1 minute each).
2. Listeners note the line they find most suspenseful.
3. Class votes for the most gripping version.
4. Board two or three of the most effective lines heard.

### Stage 5: Language focus, analysis (12 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To raise awareness of the structures professional writers use to create suspense.
**Interaction:** Groups, whole class

1. Give out Handout 3. Groups compare Version A and Version B from Handout 1 and complete the analysis table: for each change, name the technique and describe the effect (6 minutes).
2. Class feedback. Build the centre of the board with the patterns.
3. Focus on accuracy: *No sooner ... than* vs *Hardly ... when*; word order after inversion; the dangling participle in Question 4.
4. Ask: *"Which of these techniques did you already use in your task? Which did nobody use?"*

### Stage 6: Language focus, practice (10 minutes)
**Stage aim:** To practise the structures accurately.
**Interaction:** Individual, then pairs

1. Students complete Handout 4, Exercise A (transformations) (5 minutes). Pairs check. Feedback.
2. **Exercise B:** pairs improve two sentences from their own task using a technique they hadn't used, then check against the style checklist.

## Differentiation

**Support**
- Give model sentences for each technique on a reference card.
- Pair weaker students with stronger writers for the task.

**Stretch**
- Students vary the point of view or use free indirect speech.
- They write the next paragraph of the story for homework, using a technique not yet practised.

## Assessment and success criteria
- The final text uses at least four techniques accurately.
- Students can name and explain each technique.
- 8 out of 10 or better on Handout 4, Exercise A.

## Homework
Write a 250-word suspense story opening. Use the style checklist and highlight each technique you use.

## Teacher's reflection
- Did the task produce interesting writing before the language focus?
- Did the language focus help students improve the second draft?

# Handout 1: Two Openings

**Version A**

> I got home at 11 pm. I opened the door. The house was dark. I turned on the light, but it didn't work. I heard a noise upstairs. I went up the stairs. A door was open. I looked inside. There was nobody there. Then the phone rang.

**Version B**

> It was almost midnight by the time I reached the house. Having fumbled for my keys in the dark, I pushed the door open and reached for the light switch. Nothing. I was about to call out when I heard it: a slow, heavy footstep, directly above my head. Never had the staircase seemed so long. I was halfway up, counting each step, holding my breath, when I noticed that my bedroom door, the door I always close, was wide open. I stepped inside. Empty. No sooner had I let out a breath than the phone began to ring.

# Answer Key

**Handout 3 (examples)**
- Participle clause: *Having fumbled for my keys in the dark* (adds detail and tension, speeds up the action)
- One-word sentence: *Nothing.* / *Empty.* (creates a shock and a pause)
- *was about to ... when*: *I was about to call out when I heard it* (interrupts the action)
- Negative inversion: *Never had the staircase seemed so long* (emphasises fear)
- Delaying: *I was halfway up ... when I noticed* (builds tension)
- *No sooner ... than*: *No sooner had I let out a breath than the phone began to ring* (a sudden twist)
1. Auxiliary + subject (*had I*) · 2. than · 3. when · 4. Dangling participle: the lights didn't lock the door. Correct: *Having locked the door, she turned off the lights.*

**Handout 4, Exercise A**
1. No sooner had I closed my eyes than the alarm went off.
2. Hardly had she left when the parcel arrived.
3. I was about to call him when the line went dead.
4. Having checked the windows, he went upstairs.
5. Never (before) had I felt so alone.
6. Only when I saw the letter did I understand what had happened.
7. Hearing the scream, he ran outside.
8. Scarcely had we started eating when the power went off.
9. Not until much later did I realise he was lying.
10. No sooner had she opened the box than she screamed.

**Common errors to watch for**

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| No sooner I had arrived than ... | No sooner had I arrived than ... |
| Hardly had she left than he came. | Hardly had she left when he came. |
| Having locked the door, the lights went out. | Having locked the door, she turned off the lights. |
| Only then I realised ... | Only then did I realise ... |
