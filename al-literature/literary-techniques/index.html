Literary Techniques

Precision of vocabulary is worth having — but only because it lets you be precise about effect. A term used without an effect attached is a wasted line.

  1. Home
  2. A/L Literature
  3. Literary Techniques

Narrative technique

Focalisation
Whose perspective the narration adopts, which may differ from who narrates.
Example: A narrator recalling childhood may focalise through the child, so that the reader understands what the child cannot.
Free indirect style
Third-person narration inhabiting a character's own words and idiom without quotation marks.
Example: It allows sympathy and irony simultaneously, and identifying it accurately is a real mark of advanced reading.
Unreliable narration
An account the reader is invited to doubt.
Example: Look for contradictions, over-insistence, and behaviour by others that the narrator's version cannot explain.
Analepsis / prolepsis
Flashback and flash-forward.
Example: Ask why the information appears here rather than in sequence. The answer is usually irony or suspense.
Frame narrative
A story contained within another story.
Example: Ask what the frame does — it usually establishes distance, or raises a question about who is authorised to tell.
Duration and ellipsis
The proportion of text to time, and what is skipped entirely.
Example: What a novel omits is as expressive as what it narrates.

Poetic technique

Volta
The turn in a poem's argument.
Example: Locating it precisely often organises the whole answer.
Enjambment / end-stopping
Whether syntax runs past the line ending.
Example: Sustained enjambment can create urgency; a sudden end-stop after it lands with weight.
Caesura
A strong pause within a line.
Example: It can enact hesitation, interruption, or a break in composure.
Metre and substitution
The underlying rhythm, and departures from it.
Example: You need not scan formally; you must notice where the rhythm is disturbed and say what the disturbance does.
Half-rhyme
Rhyme that is nearly but not quite exact.
Example: It can produce unease, or a refusal of the neatness that full rhyme promises.
Conceit
An extended, elaborate comparison sustained through a passage.
Example: Ask whether the comparison is strained deliberately — a strained conceit may be part of the point.
Apostrophe
Direct address to someone absent, dead, or to an abstraction.
Example: It creates urgency, and often exposes the speaker's isolation.

Dramatic and rhetorical technique

Dramatic irony
The audience knows what a character does not.
Example: Always say what it makes the audience feel and why the dramatist wants that feeling here.
Peripeteia and anagnorisis
Reversal of fortune, and the moment of recognition.
Example: In many tragedies the two are separated deliberately, and the gap between them is where the suffering lies.
Stagecraft
Use of the physical theatre — space, movement, entrances, objects, light, silence.
Example: Stage directions are text and may be quoted.
Subtext
What is meant but not said.
Example: Evasions, changes of subject and silences are evidence.
Anaphora
Repeated opening words across successive clauses.
Example: Builds insistence and rhythm; marks language as public and oratorical.
Antithesis
Balanced opposition within a sentence.
Example: Makes a claim sound reasoned and inevitable — which is worth interrogating.
Bathos
An unintended or deliberate fall from the elevated to the trivial.
Example: Deliberate bathos is a powerful deflating device; ask whether the deflation is aimed at the subject or the speaker.
Litotes
Affirming by denying the opposite — not unkind.
Example: Often produces restraint or dry humour, and can suggest emotion held deliberately in check.
